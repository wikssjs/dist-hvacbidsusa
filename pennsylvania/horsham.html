Homeowners who already have ducts in good condition will save thousands on equipment changeouts. Refrigerant rules and your existing system

A new refrigerant standard applies to all newly manufactured residential HVAC systems: lower-GWP refrigerants such as R-454B and R-32 are now the industry standard. However, if you own a working system that uses R-410A, you are not required to replace it. The more significant change is economic—R-410A remains legal to purchase and use for service, but as manufacturers scale down production and supply tightens, the cost of recharging a leaky R-410A system is likely to trend upward over the next several years.

For a Horsham homeowner, this simply means your existing R-410A system can still be repaired, maintained, and recharged, provided a certified technician handles any refrigerant work. The shifting market does affect the repair-versus-replace calculation: if your system develops a refrigerant leak, you may face a steeper service bill for R-410A refills, which can tip the scales toward replacement, especially on equipment more than 10 years old or with a known history of leaks. Still, having an R-410A system is not a code violation, nor does any rule make existing equipment illegal to operate. The refrigerant transition only limits what can be installed new and affects long-run costs of owning an older system—not what remains in use.

Duct condition, leakage and sealing

Ductwork in older Horsham-area homes, especially those built before 1980, is often a bigger source of wasted energy than the age or efficiency of the central heating and cooling equipment itself. Many houses in suburban parts of Abington, Cheltenham, and Upper Dublin have retrofit ductwork—sometimes hastily added to serve air conditioning decades after the house was built. This can mean undersized return ducts, poorly insulated supply runs, and significant leakage where duct joints were never sealed properly.

A duct blaster test measures exactly how much air is escaping from your duct system, pinpointing leaks that rob your system of capacity and efficiency. In homes with high heating and cooling costs, or rooms that never seem to get comfortable, duct leakage is a likely culprit. Sealing existing ducts with mastic or aerosolized sealants often offers more return on investment than overspending on high-SEER equipment. Full replacement is sometimes called for if ducts are rusted, crushed, or impossible to access for repair.

Ducts running through unconditioned attics or crawlspaces, a feature in some post-war homes, lose cooled or heated air quickly. If your returns were sized only for a furnace (common from the 1950s to 1970s), upgrading to central air or a heat pump will highlight those limitations. The bottom line: test and address duct leakage before installing new HVAC equipment—don’t throw good money after bad by replacing a furnace or AC on a leaky delivery system.

What a Pennsylvania HVAC contractor must hold

Pennsylvania does not issue a statewide HVAC contractor license for residential work. Instead, regulation depends mainly on your municipality. Most towns in Montgomery County—including Horsham—enforce their own requirements for mechanical permits and inspections when replacing or installing major HVAC equipment. These permits ensure installations meet the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code, which is based on the International Residential Code.

All contractors taking on work over $500 must hold a Home Improvement Contractor registration from the state Attorney General’s office. You can and should verify this before signing any contract. If your installation requires refrigerant handling, federal law requires the technician to hold EPA Section 608 certification. Always ask to see proof; reputable firms are ready for this question. For employers, Pennsylvania law mandates workers’ compensation insurance—check a contractor’s compliance in the WCAIS system before they start work on your property. Mechanical permits are your guarantee the work will be inspected for code compliance, not just completed with whatever material the contractor had on hand.

Humidity and air quality in Horsham homes

Horsham’s cold winters and humid summers mean homeowners face both dry-air problems for several months and muggy indoor conditions midsummer. An oversized air conditioner—a common shortcut in replacements for older homes—can make summer discomfort worse by cooling the air rapidly but failing to remove enough humidity. Proper sizing and attention to duct performance are usually more effective than add-on dehumidifiers for most homes in this area.

Whole-house humidifiers offer some relief for winter dryness, but if they’re neglected, they can quickly breed scale, bacteria, and mold inside ductwork. They need annual cleaning and water panel replacement to stay effective and safe. For filtration, a MERV-11 to MERV-13 filter captures most allergens without sacrificing your blower’s airflow. Going higher (MERV-14+) is rarely worthwhile unless your system was sized for it. Tighter newer homes—those with upgraded windows and lots of insulation—sometimes need mechanical ventilation to avoid stale air and moisture buildup, but for most Horsham homes, spot ventilation (like bath fans) handles it well. Skip sales pitches on “air scrubbers” and “UV systems” unless your allergies or health actually demand it; they add cost but little measurable benefit in normal houses.

What construction era means for your system

Nearly half of Horsham’s houses were built before 1980, so there’s no one-size-fits-all answer when planning an HVAC upgrade. If your home is pre-1960s, there’s a good chance it originally had no ductwork at all, only radiators or baseboard heat. Adding ducts for central AC or a heat pump can mean major drywall work and expense—sometimes $10,000–$20,000 all in for a full system. If your house dates from the big post-war suburban expansions—model homes from the 1970s to the 1990s—it likely has ductwork, but it was often sized for heating alone, not for air conditioning or high-efficiency heat pumps. That can mean undersized returns, noisy operation, and hot or cold spots if not evaluated before replacement.

Builder-grade systems from the late 1990s or early 2000s are now at the end of their design life even if they’re still running. Original windows and insulation tended to be only modest by today’s standards, so efficiency gains from better equipment and air sealing can be dramatic. Finally, electric service in older homes (especially 100-amp panels) can be a hidden roadblock to all-electric conversions—worth checking before signing up for a heat pump or adding central AC. Chimneys and flues on gas systems should always be evaluated during replacement, as old liners can be a safety risk with a new, high-efficiency furnace.

Efficiency ratings and what they mean for your bill

Most homeowners see SEER2 and HSPF2 ratings and want to know what they actually mean for bills. SEER2 (Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio 2) grades how efficiently an air conditioner or heat pump cools—higher is better, but only up to a point. HSPF2 (Heating Seasonal Performance Factor 2) shows heating efficiency for heat pumps. These “2” scores replaced earlier ratings in 2023 and are not apples-to-apples with numbers found in old articles or brochures.

With about 4,800 heating degree days and just 1,450 cooling degree days, Montgomery County is clearly heating-dominated. That means HSPF2 ratings will affect your electric bills more than chasing top-tier SEER2. For most Horsham homes, paying for AC above 18–20 SEER2 has a long payback—diminishing returns unless you run AC constantly all summer. With heat pumps, spend for at least HSPF2 8–10, but past HSPF2 11, the efficiency premium rarely pays you back before the next system is due. Consider middle-of-the-road, reliable equipment before splurging on models claiming ultra-high efficiency, especially if your ductwork is original or returns are marginal. The rest of your system will limit the savings you actually see.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in Horsham

How does southeastern Pennsylvania's climate affect system sizing? Southeastern Pennsylvania, including Horsham, has more than three times as many heating degree days as cooling degree days, meaning winter heat demand is much greater than summer cooling. This shapes both the type and the sizing of HVAC systems. For example, if you’re considering a heat pump for both heating and cooling, the system needs to be sized primarily for heating loads, not just the air conditioning need. Oversized AC systems—sometimes chosen to “beat the heat”—can actually make your home less comfortable by failing to remove humidity properly. A reputable contractor will size your system using local climate data and your home's insulation and window quality, leading to better comfort and efficiency. This often leads to options in the $8,900–$19,000 range for a properly sized, ducted heat pump installation.
Can I service my R-410A system after the refrigerant change? Yes, in Horsham and the rest of Pennsylvania, you can still service and maintain any working R-410A system. The new refrigerant rules only apply to new HVAC equipment being installed—not existing systems in the field. Technicians can continue to recharge and repair R-410A units, but expect the cost of that refrigerant to climb over time as it’s phased down. If your older system develops a leak, you could face repairs edging toward the $5,600–$13,500 range that’s common for new central air equipment, which may affect the repair-or-replace decision. But your R-410A system isn’t becoming illegal or obsolete overnight.
Who inspects HVAC work in Horsham? In Horsham Township, HVAC work that involves significant equipment replacement or new installations requiring electrical or mechanical connections will require a mechanical permit. Local township code officials—rather than the state—perform the inspection, ensuring the work complies with the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code. Minor service, such as filter changes or basic thermostat repairs, typically doesn’t need a permit in Montgomery County. For a full AC or heat pump system (in the $8,900–$19,000 range), inspection by local officials is the norm and part of your contractor’s responsibility.
How long does HVAC installation take in Horsham? The timeline for HVAC installation in Horsham depends on the scope of the work. For straightforward replacements—like swapping a central AC or gas furnace using existing ducts—the process usually takes a full day to a day and a half. If you’re adding a new ducted heat pump or full duct system to a house that never had them, expect at least three to five days, especially if new return runs, venting, or significant electrical upgrades are needed. Inspections coordinated with township officials can sometimes add a day, but most jobs finish this phase quickly. For just a ductless mini-split in one room, installation can be done in under a day, with prices starting around $3,600–$7,800.
Can a heat pump heat my house without backup? In Horsham’s climate, a modern “cold-climate” heat pump can handle the bulk of your winter heating needs, with local winter design temperatures around 14 °F. Systems with HSPF2 ratings of 9–11 are designed to operate efficiently even when it’s below freezing. That said, most houses older than 1980 in this area still have some form of backup heat—electric strip heaters or a gas furnace—especially for those rare nights when it dips into the single digits. For most homes, a cold-climate model in the $14,500–$19,000 range is capable of stand-alone heating, but an emergency backup is still smart, and required by code in many cases.
How much does ductwork add to an HVAC installation? Many Horsham homes built before 1980, or those originally designed with baseboard or radiator heat, lack existing ductwork suitable for today’s HVAC systems. If you’re installing central air or a heat pump, newly installed ducts are a significant expense—usually between $10,000–$20,000 for a full system. This includes new supply and return runs, insulation, register installation, and sometimes drywall or attic work. If your current ducts just need sealing or resizing, that can reduce the cost considerably. For some homes, a ductless mini-split—at $3,600–$7,800 per zone—makes more sense for rooms without easy duct access.
How much does a new HVAC system cost in Horsham? In Horsham, the cost for a new central AC or heat pump varies based on the existing condition of your ducts and your heating needs. For central AC replacement using existing ductwork, expect to pay between $5,600–$13,500. For a ducted heat pump that covers both heating and cooling, prices run from $8,900–$19,000, depending on capacity, efficiency, and electrical upgrades. Ductwork is a major cost variable: if you need a new full system, add $10,000–$20,000 to the project. Always get multiple written estimates and make sure every quote includes permit, inspection, and warranty details specific to Montgomery County.
Is an annual maintenance plan worth it? For most Horsham homeowners, an annual maintenance plan can be worthwhile, especially as almost half of the homes are decades old and subject to normal wear on equipment. While some plans are no better than a prepaid tune-up, good ones include filter changes, system checks, and a priority response if your system breaks under warranty. Well-maintained equipment can prevent unexpected repairs and keep energy bills down, getting you more life out of your system. Typical plan costs are a fraction of a single major repair, and much less than the price of a new heat pump system—which sits in the $8,900–$19,000 range. Make sure your contract spells out what’s included and doesn’t lock you into unneeded extras.
Do I need ENERGY STAR equipment to qualify for incentives? In Pennsylvania, some utility and state programs require ENERGY STAR-rated equipment for any incentives or rebates, but not every rebate in Montgomery County is tied strictly to ENERGY STAR labels. However, in practice, most heat pumps and central AC units that qualify for rebates will also meet ENERGY STAR minimums. Before making a final decision, check which incentives are offered and what their requirements are. Keep in mind, you’ll often pay a premium—sometimes bumping your installed cost from $8,900–$19,000 for a standard unit up to the higher end for qualifying models—but the rebates and long-term savings can offset this upfront cost for many Horsham homeowners, especially those with higher electric bills.
