Repair or replace your Lower Merion system?

Not every HVAC problem in Lower Merion is a reason to replace your system, even with aged equipment. If your system is less than 10 years old and the issue is a failed component—like a capacitor, contactor, or igniter—a simple repair will often get you several more years of service. Generally, if the repair costs less than a third of replacement, and efficiency gains from newer equipment aren’t dramatic due to our moderate number of cooling days, you’re usually better off repairing.

Start considering replacement if your unit is past 15 years or the repair gets anywhere near half the cost of a new installation. Two failures make replacement non-negotiable: a cracked heat exchanger, which is a direct safety risk, and a compressor failure in an out-of-warranty unit at or past its expected life.

Before replacing a system just because your bills are high, have your ductwork and home envelope (insulation and air sealing) checked for leaks. Inefficient ducts and drafts are often the true culprits behind high energy use, not just the age of your equipment.

What size system does a Lower Merion home need?

Sizing is the number one place HVAC contractors get it wrong in Lower Merion—and it’s not in your favor. An oversized furnace or air conditioner will cycle on and off too rapidly, never reaching peak efficiency, creating uncomfortable swings in temperature and leaving excess humidity in the air during summer. It’s not a cushion; it’s a problem that shortens equipment life and raises bills.

The right way to size is with a Manual J load calculation, which takes into account your home’s insulation, window quality, orientation, infiltration, and actual living space. Pennsylvania’s winter design temperature in this region is 14°F, so a proper load calculation ensures your system heats reliably even on the coldest nights. “Rule of thumb” sizing by square footage fails in these mixed-age houses where not all rooms are equally insulated or draft-free.

If your contractor is sizing off the old equipment’s nameplate, they’re just guessing—possibly copying someone else’s bad work. Always ask: “Did you run a load calculation, and can I see it?” A good contractor will document this, especially for older homes in Lower Merion where duct capacity and insulation levels vary.

Separating installers from equipment sellers

In Lower Merion and surrounding Montgomery County, installation quality is what makes or breaks a heating or cooling system. Two identical systems—one installed with real attention to airflow, duct sealing, and system balancing, the other just “put in”—will perform as if they’re different machines. Many companies in this saturated metro market focus on brands and features, not details of installation that determine actual comfort and long-term cost.

Insist on verified credentials: check the contractor’s registration through the Home Improvement Contractor registry, make sure the technicians handling refrigerant carry federal EPA Section 608 certification, and ask for proof of workers' compensation. Look for a formal load calculation (not a guess), and quotes that list specific model numbers, not just system size. Humidity and air quality in Lower Merion homes

In Lower Merion and all of southeastern Pennsylvania, you face a classic Northeast swing: dry indoor air in the winter and muggy, humid summers. With our moderate freeze-thaw cycle and about 4,800 heating degree days to 1,450 cooling degree days, you’re more likely to fight winter dryness and summer stickiness than true indoor air pollution.

Most comfort issues stem from improper system sizing—a too-large air conditioner won’t run long enough to pull humidity out of the air. Whole-house humidifiers can help in the cold months, but need regular cleaning to prevent mold and mineral buildup. High-efficiency filters (MERV 11–13) are great for air quality but may stress the blower if your ductwork is restrictive, so always ask your contractor about compatibility.

Mechanical ventilation is increasingly important in newly renovated or tightly sealed homes, but most homes here don’t need elaborate air purification gadgets. Add-ons like UV lights and stand-alone ionizers are routinely oversold; their benefit rarely justifies the extra cost for a typical Lower Merion home. Focus on getting the basics—good filtration, balanced humidity, and proper system sizing—done right first.

What a Pennsylvania HVAC contractor must hold

For residential HVAC work in Pennsylvania—including Lower Merion—there is no statewide HVAC license. Instead, contractors doing jobs over $500 must hold Home Improvement Contractor registration from the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. Mechanical work is regulated at the municipal level: Lower Merion Township issues its own mechanical permits, and municipalities are primarily responsible for code enforcement, following the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code based on the IRC.

Anyone handling refrigerant—installing or servicing air conditioners or heat pumps—must have federal EPA Section 608 certification. This is non-negotiable and required everywhere, whether the state licenses HVAC work or not.

You can verify a contractor’s insurance (including mandatory workers’ compensation for employers) using the Pennsylvania WCAIS system. For consumer protection, state law requires a written contract for any home improvement over $500, a three-business-day right to cancel, and you cannot waive your insurance deductible.

Permit requirements depend on the work: in Lower Merion, installing or replacing an HVAC system usually requires a mechanical permit, but simple filter changes or thermostat battery swaps do not. Always make sure your contractor gets permits and code inspections where required for major work.

How is a tax credit different from a rebate? In Pennsylvania, and specifically in Lower Merion, a tax credit is money you get back when you file your federal or state tax return—usually a set percentage of the system cost, applied against the taxes you owe. You won't see this money immediately at purchase. In contrast, a rebate is a discount provided either directly by the utility or manufacturer—sometimes through PECO Energy Company—or by government programs, and you typically receive the money shortly after the purchase, often as a check or instant discount. For example, if you install a ductless mini-split for $3,900–$8,500, you might qualify for a $300 utility rebate up front, plus a tax credit at the end of the year. Always confirm eligibility with both your HVAC contractor and the utility before making assumptions about either.

What does HSPF2 measure? HSPF2 stands for Heating Seasonal Performance Factor (version 2), and it’s the efficiency metric for air source heat pumps operating in our heating-heavy climate like Lower Merion. It measures the total heat output of a system (in BTUs) over the entire heating season, divided by the energy it uses (in watt-hours). Higher HSPF2 numbers mean greater efficiency—most ducted units installed locally rate between 8–10, with “cold climate” models reaching 11. This is useful in Lower Merion, where more homes are now considering heat pumps for main heat. While a system with higher HSPF2 costs more up front (often $16,000–$20,500 installed for cold-climate versions), it pays off in lower winter heating bills.

Do I need dual fuel in southeastern Pennsylvania? In Lower Merion and the broader southeastern Pennsylvania region, dual fuel systems—where a heat pump is paired with a gas furnace—are popular for their balance of efficiency and comfort. Our winter design temperature of 14°F and long heating season (4,800 heating degree days) mean that a heat pump alone will lose efficiency and comfort during cold snaps. Dual fuel lets the system run efficiently during most winter weather but switches to gas when it gets very cold. While a basic ducted heat pump system can be installed for $9,700–$20,500, dual fuel setups add to this but often pay back in consistent comfort and utility savings. Always confirm if your existing gas service and electrical system can support the added load.
