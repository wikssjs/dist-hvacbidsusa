HVAC costs in Scranton — 2026

The local climate is heating-dominated, with 6,100 heating degree days and only 850 cooling degree days. In plain terms: heating is the main expense, and system choices must reflect that. Air conditioning is important but rarely the chief load. In Scranton’s saturated HVAC market, prices reflect a full installation by a contractor registered for projects over $500, per state law.

Mechanical permits for HVAC work in Scranton

In Pennsylvania, HVAC contractors are not licensed statewide for residential work. Instead, contractors performing jobs over $500 must hold a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration issued by the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. Scranton and neighboring municipalities regulate residential work at the local level—specifically, the City of Scranton Licensing, Inspections & Permits Department handles code enforcement inside the city. This means that your HVAC contractor needs a municipal permit for nearly all HVAC replacements or new installations in Scranton, and that work will be inspected locally for compliance with the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), which follows the International Residential Code framework.

No matter who you hire, anyone handling refrigerant (installation, removal, or topping off) must have federal EPA Section 608 certification. This applies regardless of state or municipal licensing and is basic job-site compliance for air conditioning and heat pump work everywhere in the U.S.

For insurance, Pennsylvania requires all employers—including HVAC companies—to carry workers’ compensation, which you can verify through the state’s WCAIS system. Before signing a contract over $500, verify both the contractor’s HIC registration and insurance. Always demand a written contract and never pay the full price up front; the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) gives you the right to cancel within three business days.

Refrigerant rules and your existing system

If you have an air conditioner or heat pump installed in the past decade, it almost certainly uses R-410A refrigerant. Under federal regulations, newly manufactured systems are shifting to lower-global-warming-potential refrigerants like R-454B and R-32. The good news for Scranton homeowners is that no law requires you to replace your existing R-410A system solely because of the refrigerant—a working system remains legal and serviceable for its lifetime.

Where this does matter is on the repair side. As the industry transitions and production of R-410A is scaled back, the cost of that refrigerant creeps up. If your system develops a leak, the repair-versus-replace equation changes not because the old unit is banned, but because topping off a leaker gets pricier each year. If your R-410A system works fine and shows no leaks, keep it. If facing expensive repairs involving a refrigerant recharge, think twice about investing heavily in it—the economics, rather than the code, make the replacement more attractive over time.

The change is about what gets installed going forward, not about prohibiting service on equipment already in homes. Plan accordingly, and don’t be rushed by scare tactics around refrigerant “bans.”

Duct condition, leakage and sealing

In Scranton and Old Forge, much of the housing stock dates to well before 1940, so ductwork varies from nonexistent to undersized to poorly sealed. If your home has ducts, expect some quirks—undersized returns, odd routing, or even switches from gravity furnaces to forced air in the last century. In older duplexes and rowhouses, ducts may have been squeezed into wall cavities, often with no sealing and plenty of leakage.

Duct leakage is not trivial here—a duct blaster test measures how much air escapes from the entire distribution system. In pre-1980 construction, it’s not rare to lose enough conditioned air through leaky ducts that the “star rating” on your equipment becomes meaningless. Sealing existing ductwork can save serious energy and improve comfort, especially if runs travel through unconditioned attics or crawlspaces.

Undersized returns are especially common: old systems sized for a furnace may not allow enough airflow for today’s high-efficiency AC or heat pumps, leading to noise, temperature differences, or system stress. It’s far better to test duct performance before replacing heating or cooling units; replacing a system without a duct test risks wasting money on oversized new equipment while still suffering from comfort problems and high bills. Retrofit means evaluating the entire distribution setup, not just the furnace or AC.

The comfort problem that isn't temperature

In Scranton’s climate—long heating season, short but muggy summers—the invisible comfort challenges are dryness in winter and humidity in summer. Heating-dominated homes can see indoor humidity drop to uncomfortable levels, drying out wood floors and skin. A whole-house humidifier can help, but it comes with maintenance: regular cleaning, water panel replacement, and the risk of mineral buildup or mold if ignored.

In summer, high humidity can make 74°F feel sticky. An oversized air conditioner—a common mistake in older homes—won’t run long enough cycles to effectively remove that humidity. The key is properly sized equipment and possibly a variable-speed system. Many indoor air quality add-ons (like UV lights or ozone generators) are oversold or deliver little for most Scranton homes, where outdoor air here is not a major problem.

If a contractor pushes a high-MERV filter, check your blower’s capability—jumping from MERV 8 to MERV 13 on an older system can choke airflow. Mechanical ventilation is a consideration only if you’ve had extensive air sealing done in a recent retrofit; most Scranton homes “leak” enough naturally without adding expensive ventilation equipment.

Keeping a Scranton system running

Nothing preserves HVAC life in Scranton homes better than routine filter changes and a little common sense. In our heating-driven market, a clogged filter strains your blower and invites dust into the system—stick to your manufacturer’s schedule, and if you adopt a higher-efficiency (MERV 11+), verify your blower can really handle it. Yearly coil cleaning matters where cottonwood fluff and dust clog outdoor units in spring.

Flush the condensate line each spring to avert water leaks, and have refrigerant charge checked with gauges rather than “topped off.” If a heat pump or AC ever needs a recharge, there’s a leak—no routine needs topping up after the first year.

Service plans in Scranton run the gamut: a solid plan covers a real tune-up and minor service—not just a filter swap. Plans that only offer “priority” appointments or push unneeded cleanings tend to be bad deals. The best time to schedule is early spring or fall—before peak breakdown season and you’re up against a long list of emergency calls.

SEER2, HSPF2 and AFUE decoded

SEER2 is a measure of cooling efficiency for air conditioners and heat pumps, reflecting real-world conditions. HSPF2 measures the heating efficiency of a heat pump over an average season, and AFUE covers how efficiently a furnace turns fuel into heat.

For Scranton and Lackawanna County’s 6,100 heating degree days and only 850 cooling degree days, the numbers that pay back are HSPF2 (for heat pumps) and AFUE (for furnaces). Chasing top-end SEER2 ratings (above 18) costs extra but recoups little in our short cooling season. Focus on a good HSPF2 (above 9 for ducted heat pumps) and a furnace AFUE of at least 95%. There’s little reason to pay for the highest SEER2 rating unless you have heavy cooling needs, which few in this county do.

Note: New test procedures mean SEER2 and HSPF2 are lower than old SEER and HSPF, so beware any comparisons with older articles—a 16 SEER2 is roughly a 17–18 SEER under the old method. Pay for efficiency where your bills are highest: heating, not cooling.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in Scranton

Should I replace the furnace and AC at the same time? If your Scranton home has a forced-air system that uses both a furnace and central AC, replacing both at once can make sense—but it isn’t always necessary. If either system is under 12 years old and working well, you don’t have to replace both. However, if your furnace or AC is reaching the end of its expected life and you’re considering a high-efficiency air source heat pump, the full system replacement brings benefits: matched efficiency and often better rebates. In Scranton, a typical AC swap runs $4,400–$10,500, while upgrading furnace and AC together usually means new duct adjustments and more labor. Custom retrofits in older homes may add to this cost. Generally, joint replacement is about reliability and potential cost savings in labor, not about code.
What is the R-410A refrigerant phase-down? The R-410A refrigerant used in most Scranton air conditioners and heat pumps is being phased down at the manufacturing level due to its high global warming potential. What this means for Pennsylvania homeowners is that new systems are shipping with other alternatives, like R-32 or R-454B. Your existing R-410A equipment isn’t being “banned”—it can still be serviced and used legally. But as supplies tighten, the cost of R-410A for repairs rises, especially if you have an old system that develops leaks. If faced with an expensive repair for an older unit, consider putting that money toward a new system ($7,100–$15,000 for a ducted heat pump) instead of sinking it into a dying model. There’s no mandate to replace a functioning system.
Why do HVAC quotes in Scranton vary so much? Scranton’s HVAC quotes often span several thousand dollars for the same type of job. This comes down to three real local factors: the age and layout of the home, condition (or existence) of ductwork, and how thorough a company is in sizing and custom-fitting retrofits to the unique older homes here. Labor rates are also affected by competition in this market, with several contractors spread through Scranton, Old Forge, and the surrounding boroughs. Another reason: scope of work. Some bids may skip duct repair or only include basic equipment; others may add needed duct sealing or electrical upgrades. For example, a full duct installation here ranges from $8,000–$16,000, depending on complexity. When comparing, always get a breakdown of work included.
How does northeastern Pennsylvania's climate affect system sizing? Our region, with 6,100 heating degree days, needs systems that can handle long, cold winters—heating is the main design load. A system sized for proper winter performance might run a little oversized for cooling, but it’s essential for homes in Scranton, where sizing mistakes hit comfort and bills hard. Older houses with drafty walls and little insulation need more capacity than a newer, tightly sealed home. Oversizing the air conditioner leads to humidity problems, while undersizing a furnace or heat pump means you’ll be cold in January. Given the predominant housing stock, custom sizing is critical—don’t settle for a one-size-fits-all bid.
Do I need to be home during installation? While it’s not always required for you to be at your Scranton home the entire time, it’s best to be present at the start and end of any HVAC installation. This ensures the installer can walk through the work area, answer questions, and review the equipment location. For a major installation, such as a full duct system and air source heat pump ($7,100–$15,000), your contractor may need access to different parts of the house throughout the day. At minimum, someone should be available by phone in case decisions come up. Being present helps avoid last-minute surprises, especially in older rowhouses where running new ducts or lines could raise unforeseen issues.
What is EPA Section 608 certification? EPA Section 608 is the federal certification required for anyone who handles refrigerants in air conditioners and heat pumps. In Scranton and across Pennsylvania, no matter a contractor’s HIC registration, only technicians with this certification can legally recover, recharge, or dispose of refrigerant. This protects the environment by ensuring proper handling and leak prevention. When you hire a contractor for any system involving refrigerant—whether a simple AC replacement or a mini-split install (which runs $2,800–$6,200)—ask to see a Section 608 card for the installer, especially if the job involves opening up sealed systems. It’s a minimum standard and not optional.
How often should I change my filter? For most Scranton homes, changing your HVAC filter every 1–3 months is plenty. The exact interval depends on your filter type (one-inch versus five-inch), whether you have pets, and your home’s air quality. Old housing stock can mean more dust circulation, especially in winter, so check monthly if you’re unsure. Running a higher MERV filter than the blower is designed for can reduce airflow and cause comfort issues. Good practice: check monthly, change quarterly unless visibly dirty or your system starts to “howl.” Filters are cheap insurance, and keeping up with them beats most “add-on” air quality gadgets in terms of measurable benefit. Most Pennsylvania maintenance plans include filter swaps.
How much does ductwork add to an HVAC installation? In the Scranton area, adding a full new duct system is rarely simple, especially in pre-1940 rowhouses and duplexes. Most full duct installations—where none exist or the old ducts have failed—cost between $8,000–$16,000 on top of the equipment. This reflects the labor and design needed to fit ducts in older walls, floors, or attics, and often the need to patch plaster or repair finishes afterward. If your current ductwork is sound, a central AC or heat pump replacement is much less costly. Always have your ducts inspected and tested before a major system replacement—retrofitting poorly designed or leaky ductwork only wastes money on new equipment and fails to fix comfort problems.
Are duct sealing and insulation eligible for incentives? Duct sealing and insulation can often qualify for utility or federal incentives, but eligibility for Scranton homeowners depends on current programs offered by PPL Electric Utilities or through broader state efforts. Sealing ducts in houses built before 1980 (most of Scranton’s housing) can greatly reduce heating costs. Sometimes, utility programs cover the cost of a blower door or duct blaster test, then partially subsidize sealing. If you’re investing in a major duct upgrade as part of a retrofit—with installations in the $8,000–$16,000 range—ask your contractor to document the work for incentive paperwork. Always check current offers before starting; programs vary year by year.
