Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, you should never treat HVAC as “permit-free” work. Pennsylvania requires HVAC contractors to hold a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration through the Pennsylvania Attorney General’s Office. For gas appliances (like furnaces and gas furnace components), the installing technician must hold an active PA gas piping license. For AC and heat pump refrigerant handling, the technician must have EPA Section 608 certification—ask to see the technician’s EPA 608 card before any refrigerant work begins.

Mechanical permits are required by most PA municipalities for HVAC replacement and new installation; however, like-for-like filter changes or thermostat swaps typically don’t require the same permitting steps. Verify permit requirements with your Glenolden township or borough office (and any additional municipal rules that apply to your address, especially on older properties). If you’re looking at rebates, check the PA Homeowner Rebate program and utility incentives (PPL Electric, PECO, Columbia Gas) for qualifying high-efficiency heat pumps and furnaces under the IRA Inflation Reduction Act programs. ENERGY STAR certified equipment may also qualify for federal tax credits up to 30% under Section 25C.

To verify a contractor, follow this step-by-step approach: (1) Confirm HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. (2) Request the technician’s EPA 608 card for refrigerant tasks. (3) Ask for a certificate of insurance showing current general liability. (4) Ask for workers’ compensation proof (WC) documentation—especially important on job sites with ductwork, electrical, or attic access. If your contractor can’t provide these quickly, find another installer before scheduling.

Should I repair or replace my HVAC system in Glenolden?

In Glenolden, “repair vs. replace” should be based on performance, age, and whether the cost of repairs is approaching replacement-level spending. However, repeated breakdowns, chronic airflow problems, or a compressor/heat exchanger failure often signal diminishing returns, especially in older homes where duct leaks can keep stressing the system. A key decision point is whether the repair costs will exceed a reasonable portion of replacement pricing. For example, central AC replacement (with existing ducts) is commonly estimated in the $6,500–$15,000 range; if repairs are repeatedly in the same neighborhood or the system is older with poor efficiency, replacement often becomes more economical over time. For heating, furnace replacement or heat pump conversions can be a larger investment (heat pump ducted installations often in the $7,000–$14,000 band). Also consider your comfort: if you can’t control humidity in summer or you feel cold drafts in winter, the “best” repair might actually be improved airflow, insulation/air sealing, and properly matched equipment. A professional load calculation and inspection of ducts/electrical wiring will usually clarify whether repair will truly solve the underlying cause.
