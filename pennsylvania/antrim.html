Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, HVAC installs aren’t just “best practice”—they’re regulated. First, confirm your contractor holds a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration through the Pennsylvania Attorney General’s Office. For gas appliances (like a gas furnace), the technician must hold an active PA gas piping licence. For air conditioning and heat pump refrigerant work, the technician should also have EPA Section 608 certification—ask to see the EPA 608 card before work begins.

Most PA municipalities require mechanical permits for HVAC replacement and new installation. Like-for-like filter changes and simple thermostat swaps usually do not require permits, but anything that involves changing equipment, venting/combustion components, ductwork changes, or electrical disconnects typically does. Verify permit requirements with your local township or borough office before your install date.

Rebates and tax incentives can reduce the effective cost. Check Pennsylvania’s Homeowner Rebate program and utility rebates (PPL Electric, PECO, Columbia Gas) for qualifying high-efficiency heat pumps and furnaces under Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) incentives. ENERGY STAR–certified equipment may qualify for federal tax credits of up to 30% under Section 25C, depending on eligibility.

To verify a contractor in Antrim: (1) search hic.attorneygeneral.gov for the PA HIC registration; (2) request the technician’s EPA 608 certification; (3) ask for a certificate of insurance and workers’ compensation documentation; and (4) confirm the permit scope—what’s covered, who files it, and when inspection is scheduled.

Heat pump vs. furnace + AC — what makes sense in Antrim?

In Antrim, the heat pump vs. furnace decision is more nuanced than it is in milder climates. Pennsylvania’s four-season pattern includes cold winters with frequent sub-freezing stretches and hot, humid summers, so you need a system that can maintain comfort without blowing up operating costs. Modern cold-climate air source heat pumps (CC-ASHP) are designed to operate efficiently down to about -13°F, which makes them viable across Franklin County. That said, Antrim’s older housing stock (52.1% built before 1980, U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024) often comes with duct leakage and insulation gaps. Those issues can reduce real-world efficiency, regardless of equipment nameplate ratings—so the duct sealing, return design, and airflow balance matter as much as whether you choose a heat pump.

Where the price difference is justified: if you’re planning to replace cooling and heating together, combining heating and cooling with a ducted heat pump can streamline controls and reduce total complexity. For instance, if a dual system (gas furnace + central AC) is quoted around $7,500–$9,000 combined, moving to a cold-climate ducted heat pump can be in the same general ballpark at roughly $7,500–$9,000—then potentially becomes more attractive because it can qualify for the federal 30% tax credit plus utility rebates when the home and equipment meet program rules. On the other hand, if you already have excellent ducts, solid insulation, and you’re staying with gas for reasons like existing infrastructure, a furnace + central AC can still make sense.

In short: if your ducts and insulation are average-to-poor, fix distribution with the install (or choose ductless/zoned options). If you want one system to handle both seasons, cold-climate heat pumps are often the best fit for Antrim—especially when you pair them with good airflow design.

How to choose a HVAC installation contractor in Antrim

Choosing the right contractor is where Antrim homeowners can prevent surprises. Start by verifying credentials. In Pennsylvania, licensing and registrations matter: check for IICRC (as applicable to the contractor’s certification claims) and confirm PA licensing status through the relevant state or industry registry your contractor references. If they won’t give you clear verification steps, that’s a red flag. For refrigerant work, ask for the technician’s EPA 608 card and request proof of insurance.

Next, get 2–3 itemized written quotes. You want a breakdown that shows labor and materials separately (equipment model/SEER2 or AFUE, duct components, electrical disconnects, refrigerant line items, and any duct sealing or return-air work). Avoid offers that only provide a lump sum without describing what’s included or excluded—especially when older ducts or insulation gaps are involved.

Read the scope line-by-line. Does the proposal include duct leakage testing or airflow balancing? Is any crawlspace or attic work included? Is permit filing included? Ask whether insurance claim documentation is available if your project is tied to a covered loss. Confirm warranty details too: you should receive a workmanship warranty length (labor coverage) and the manufacturer warranty for equipment and parts. Finally, schedule and payment matter: never pay more than 10–15% upfront, and hold back payment until the job is complete and the system is operational and tested. Get a written arrival estimate and a completion window before the first day of work.
