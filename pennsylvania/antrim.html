The refrigerant change and what it means for repairs

As of 2025, all new residential HVAC systems manufactured for the U.S. use lower global-warming-potential (GWP) refrigerants—R-454B or R-32—instead of R-410A, as part of a national effort to phase down high-GWP HFCs. This has no impact on the legality or serviceability of your current R-410A system in Antrim, and there is absolutely no requirement to replace functioning equipment just because it uses an “old” refrigerant.

If you have a working R-410A heat pump or AC, you can continue to use it as long as parts and refrigerant remain available. For the foreseeable future, R-410A can still be purchased and used for servicing existing equipment. Where you’ll notice a change is in repair costs on anything with a significant refrigerant leak. As production and import of R-410A wind down, the price of recharging older systems will trend upward in Franklin County, shifting the math on whether to repair or replace if a major refrigerant-side repair is needed after 2025.

If your system is cooling or heating effectively and not leaking, there’s no economic or regulatory reason to replace it solely for refrigerant type. For installations after 2025, all new equipment will be factory-charged with the new refrigerants, requiring compatible tools and handling by certified technicians, but that’s an installer’s problem—not the homeowner’s. The bottom line: service what you have, but weigh the cost of major refrigerant repairs carefully from 2025 onward.

Maintenance that actually extends system life

In Antrim’s heating-heavy climate, neglecting maintenance puts the biggest strain on equipment during deep winters and humid summers. Change your air filter every 1–3 months (or as needed) and match the MERV rating to your blower motor—too high a rating on an older blower can starve airflow and shorten system life. For most older ducted systems from the ranch and split-level era, a MERV 8–11 pleated filter balances capture and airflow.

Coil cleaning, inside and out, is worth doing every season—dust, pet hair, and Franklin County pollen all reduce heat transfer. Make sure the condensate drain is clear in spring and summer. When it comes to refrigerant, there’s no such thing as a “top-off”: if your system is low, it has a leak, and just adding refrigerant is not maintenance, it’s wasting money.

Annual service plans can be good peace of mind if they include a full cleaning, coil check, and real inspection—not just a check-up. Skip plans that only offer priority scheduling and a “quick look” for a premium. The best value comes in scheduling maintenance for early fall or mid-spring, before peak season hits and contractors in Antrim get booked out with emergency calls.

Permits, licensing and inspections in Antrim

Pennsylvania does not require a state-issued HVAC trade license for residential work. Instead, residential HVAC installation in Antrim falls under local regulation and enforcement. Contractors working on projects over $500 must carry Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration, which you can check online at the official state registry. You should also confirm your contractor’s workers’ compensation insurance using the WCAIS system.

The federal EPA Section 608 certification is required for any refrigerant handling, so always verify that the lead installer (not just the company owner) is certified. Municipal mechanical permits are standard for installs and replacements in most Franklin County municipalities. In the Borough of Chambersburg, a permit is almost always required for any central HVAC installation or replacement—other townships may have their own permitting processes or requirements, but all must follow the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code for residential work. Most simple filter changes and thermostat swaps proceed without permits outside municipal boundaries, but full system jobs do not.

After installation, a final municipal inspection ensures code compliance. When interviewing contractors in Antrim or greater Franklin County, ask exactly who pulls the permit and coordinates inspection—this question alone is a basic test of professionalism.

Running the numbers on Antrim's climate

To compare HVAC running costs in Antrim, you have to factor in Franklin County’s 5,200 heating degree days (HDD) and 1,250 cooling degree days (CDD), typical for south-central Pennsylvania where we see deep winter cold and warm, humid summers. Your actual annual expense depends on the efficiency of your equipment, your home’s insulation, and your utility’s rates—West Penn Power for electric and UGI Utilities for natural gas in many areas.

Here’s how to break it down: Each system’s efficiency rating (AFUE for furnaces, HSPF2 for heat pumps, SEER2 for AC) tells you the percent of fuel converted to heat or cooling. Multiply your home’s annual heating or cooling load (estimated using degree days, system capacity, and home size) by your local energy rate, which is found on your electric or gas bill.

For example, for heating: if your home’s heating load is 50 million BTU, with a 95% AFUE furnace, you’ll use about 52.6 million BTU of gas yearly (50 ÷ 0.95). For a heat pump with 9 HSPF2, delivering 9 BTU/watt-hour, you’d need about 5,555 kilowatt-hours yearly. Multiply by your electric rate—shown as “$/kWh” on your West Penn Power bill—to get the actual cost.

But energy cost arithmetic means little if your ductwork is leaky or undersized. A high-end heat pump strapped to 40-year-old ducts rarely outperforms a mid-grade furnace on new, tight ductwork. Operating cost math works when the distribution system and envelope aren’t sabotaging your equipment—so include duct and air sealing in your evaluation.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in Antrim

What is SEER2 and how is it different from SEER?

SEER2 is the updated efficiency standard for air conditioners and heat pumps, replacing the older SEER measurement as of 2023. Both measure how efficiently an air conditioner turns electricity into cooling, but SEER2 uses tougher, more realistic test procedures that better reflect typical ductwork and installation conditions—especially important for Antrim’s mix of older, sometimes leaky ducts. For example, a system rated at 20 SEER2 beats a 20 SEER system, because it’s tested under higher, real-world static pressure. These new standards mean equipment labeled at, say, 16–20 SEER2, such as those priced at $5,100–$12,000 for a central AC replacement here, will perform closer to published ratings in your actual Franklin County home. Bottom line: SEER2 makes “apples-to-apples” comparisons more meaningful for local buyers.

Is a high-efficiency system worth the extra cost here?

In Antrim, where our 5,200 heating degree days stack up a substantial winter load, a high-efficiency heat pump or furnace can often pay back its higher cost in fuel or electric savings. Especially if you’re upgrading from something built before 2000, new equipment with efficiency ratings like 9–11 HSPF2 or 95%+ AFUE is worth considering. Still, don’t pay top dollar if your ducts are old and leaky—a high-efficiency system strapped to bad ductwork won’t deliver savings. On average, replacing with a high-efficiency ducted heat pump runs $8,100–$17,500 installed. Whether it pencils out depends on energy prices, your home’s insulation, and getting the right size and commissioning. Ask your contractor to run actual cost comparisons based on your current energy bills—don’t buy just for the sticker stats.

Do older Antrim homes need ductwork replaced?

Over half of Antrim's homes were built before 1980, so many have decades-old ductwork that may be poorly sealed, undersized, or simply rusted out. While not all ductwork needs total replacement, a full inspection during HVAC replacement is strongly recommended. Sometimes, just resealing and minor fixes will do; in other cases, especially with systems from the 50s–70s ranch and split-level era, ducts are so corroded or inefficient that replacement makes sense. Full duct system installs typically run $9,100–$18,500 in Franklin County. Replacing or upgrading also lets you optimize for today’s equipment and airflow needs, which can lead to real energy and comfort gains.

Does my utility offer a heat pump rebate in Antrim?

West Penn Power, the primary electric utility serving Antrim, regularly runs incentives for high-efficiency heat pump installs through state energy programs, especially when replacing electric resistance, oil, or propane heat. Rebates and incentives change year by year, but recent programs have helped lower the upfront cost of systems in the $8,100–$17,500 range for ducted heat pumps or the $3,200–$7,100 range for ductless single zones. Always check both your utility’s and Pennsylvania’s statewide programs before signing a contract—your contractor should help with paperwork, but you can also check the utility’s website for “HVAC rebates” or call their customer service for the latest offers.

How often should I change my filter?

For most Antrim homes, changing your HVAC air filter every 1–3 months is the safest bet—especially in our heating-dominated climate where systems run long hours in winter. If you have pets, allergies, or live near fields (with dust or pollen blowing in), err toward monthly changes. Use a pleated filter with a MERV rating between 8 and 11 unless your installer recommends otherwise for your particular blower. A clogged filter forces your furnace or heat pump to work harder, potentially leading to breakdowns or reduced efficiency, even on equipment costing $13,000–$17,500. Mark your calendar and check during each season change—your system (and bills) will thank you.

Replacing a gas furnace with matching central AC in Antrim generally runs $5,100–$12,000, depending on system size, ductwork needs, and local permitting. In rural townships without natural gas, switching to a heat pump or dual-fuel setup is more common, given the high price of propane or oil. Costs rise if your ductwork is in poor condition or needs replacing, sometimes pushing the total toward the upper end of the range. How do I verify an HVAC contractor's insurance?

Homeowners in Antrim can—and should—verify that any HVAC contractor they hire carries up-to-date workers' compensation insurance, which is mandatory for all employers in Pennsylvania. Do this by searching the company name or policy number in the WCAIS online system (wcais.pa.gov). For bigger jobs over $500, also confirm Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration via hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Don’t just take verbal assurances or a paper certificate handed over at estimate time—insurance status can and does lapse, and reputable contractors will be happy to provide the documentation or links. Can a heat pump heat my house without backup?

In Antrim’s climate, a properly sized cold-climate heat pump can usually heat most homes for the majority of the winter, even with a design temp around 12 °F and 5,200 heating degree days per year. Modern units maintain surprisingly strong capacity down to 10–15 °F, and only drop off in the rare single-digit cold snaps. If your house is decently insulated and you select equipment with an HSPF2 rating near the top end of the scale (for example, a system in the $13,000–$17,500 range), backup heat may run only a handful of days per year. Still, most installs in Franklin County include electric strip or gas backup for peace of mind during those extremes. Don’t skip a full load calculation and envelope check when sizing for full-electric heat—it matters more than any brochure performance claim.

How far ahead should I schedule in Antrim?

If you need heat in a hurry, some contractors offer emergency replacement, but you’ll have fewer choices and pay a premium. Plan ahead if you can, and you’ll have more leverage and a better outcome.
