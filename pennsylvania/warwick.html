SEER2, HSPF2 and AFUE decoded

SEER2 stands for Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio 2, and it measures how efficiently a central air conditioner or heat pump cools over a standard season. HSPF2 is Heating Seasonal Performance Factor 2, rating how efficiently a heat pump heats. AFUE is Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency, used for furnaces—how much fuel energy becomes usable heat.

For Warwick and most of Lancaster County, with 5,200 heating degree days and just 1,250 cooling degree days, heating efficiency (HSPF2 or AFUE) will save you more over time than top-end cooling efficiency. SEER2 and HSPF2 numbers were updated in 2023, so older SEER or HSPF numbers look higher but are not apples-to-apples. Don’t get thrown by outdated numbers in an article or an old quote.

You’ll see SEER2 ratings for new cooling equipment starting at 14 (legally required), but the meaningful difference in power bills flattens out above 18–20 SEER2, given our moderate but not punishing summers. Meanwhile, HSPF2 for heat pumps starts around 8 and goes up to 11 on cold-climate models—the higher, the better for winter bills. But paying for a super-high SEER2 system is seldom a good use of money in Warwick, where your savings from heating efficiency outpace what you might save on summer electric bills. Your best bet is to focus spending on HSPF2 or AFUE performance, up to the point where the extra cost pays back inside ten years; anything beyond that is mostly salesmanship.

Refrigerant rules and your existing system

There’s been a big shift recently in refrigerants: new residential systems now use R-454B or R-32, which are lower-GWP and more environmentally friendly. That’s because the industry is phasing down older high-GWP refrigerants like R-410A in new equipment. If you have an existing R-410A system in Warwick, you’re not suddenly out of compliance or facing a mandate to replace your equipment. Your system remains both legal and fully serviceable for the foreseeable future.

The practical change is financial: as new systems stop using R-410A, future supplies will tighten and the cost of recharging a leaking system will climb. This doesn’t matter for small top-offs or normal maintenance, but if your older system springs a leak, the economics shift toward replacement rather than an expensive repair. Still, there is no “ban.” A well-functioning R-410A system should be serviced as needed; you do not have to replace a working unit just because newer refrigerants exist. In short: keep your system running until repairs become uneconomical, then shop for new equipment that meets the updated refrigerant requirements.

Mechanical permits for HVAC work in Warwick

Pennsylvania does not issue a statewide HVAC trade license. Instead, residential HVAC work is handled locally. In Lancaster city, replacing or installing central equipment typically requires a mechanical permit and inspection through the city’s Bureau of Code Compliance. Surrounding townships run their own code enforcement offices. For simpler work—like filter changes or thermostat tweaks—no permit is needed across Lancaster County homes.

All residential work must conform to the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), which is based on the International Residential Code (IRC). For projects over $500, contractors must carry a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration issued by the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. This is not a technical license, but it is a basic consumer protection and contract requirement. You can verify registration status on their public registry (hic.attorneygeneral.gov).

EPA Section 608 certification is federally mandated for anyone handling refrigerant, so check that your installer’s technicians have it. Pennsylvania also requires all employers to have workers’ compensation insurance. Use the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov) to verify coverage for employers you’re considering. Always insist on written contracts, and know your three-day cancellation right under state law.

Separating installers from equipment sellers

In Warwick and the greater Lancaster County market, how your HVAC system gets installed matters more than which brand logo is on the box. Installation quality determines system durability, efficiency, and comfort—brand differences are real but usually smaller than contractor technique, especially as most equipment comes off the same handful of production lines.

Make sure your contractor is HIC-registered for jobs over $500 (verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov). The technician charging or evacuating refrigerant lines must carry EPA Section 608 certification. Demand proof the company has current workers’ comp insurance, which you can check using WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). Expect a load calculation (Manual J or equivalent), not a “rule of thumb” sizing guess. Any solid bid should specify exact model numbers, not just equipment tonnage. Ask if they document airflow and refrigerant charge at startup—a step known as commissioning.

What each system actually costs to run in Warwick

The cost to run your HVAC system in Warwick depends on three main things: how efficient your equipment is (SEER2, HSPF2, or AFUE), how much heating and cooling your house needs (degree days), and your rates for electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, or propane. For reference, PPL Electric Utilities is the main supplier for electric in this area, so your rate per kWh is listed on your monthly bill—use your own bill for the actual number, as it varies by plan and usage.

To estimate annual operating costs: Start by converting your system’s efficiency rating into a “cost per unit” delivered to your home. For heating, the number of heating degree days (5,200 annually here) and winter design temp (12 °F) indicate you’ll spend far more on heating than cooling over a year. For a heat pump rated at 9 HSPF2, figure roughly how many kWh per year it will need using your heating square footage, insulation, and air leakage.

Here’s a sample method: If your bill says $0.15 per kWh, and your heat pump pulls 10,000 kWh a year, that’s $1,500. Compare this to an older fuel oil furnace (with say 80% AFUE); factor local oil price and your fuel-use history. For air conditioning, multiply the cooling degree days (1,250) by tonnage (cooling load), and divide by SEER2 to get estimated kWh usage for the cooling months. Always compare using your own bills—the math above is only a frame. If your home’s ductwork is badly sealed or uninsulated, even a high-end heat pump can perform worse than a basic furnace or AC. Getting ductwork checked is not optional for real savings.

Keeping a Warwick system running

The basics matter most: change your filter on schedule with the right MERV rating for your blower, not just the one with the biggest “allergen” claim. Many Warwick homes built before 1980 have ductwork sized for low-to-moderate resistance; too high a MERV will strain your blower and burn out motors. Clean your system’s coils at least once a year, especially if there’s construction dust or pets. Check the condensate line to avoid clogs and water damage, especially after the heating or cooling season.

For systems with refrigerant: do not “top up” charge unless there’s an identified leak. A system that needs refrigerant has a loss—the root cause needs to be found and fixed, not just patched with more refrigerant. This is especially crucial as older R-410A becomes more expensive to recharge. As for annual service plans, they can be worth it if you get real annual maintenance (inspection, coil cleaning, performance check) and priority service. Be wary of plans that are little more than a yearly filter change and a push for add-on sales. The sweet spot for scheduling maintenance: March–April or September–October, before extremes hit and before emergency calls flood every local contractor’s calendar.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in Warwick

Is it worth switching from oil or propane to a heat pump?

In Warwick and surrounding Lancaster County, many rural homes still use oil or propane because natural gas lines are spotty beyond core townships. Air source heat pumps have become reliable enough—even in a heating-heavy climate (5,200 heating degree days)—that the switch can pay off, especially if your home is decently insulated. With current pricing, an installed ducted heat pump runs $8,200–$17,500, but the savings over oil or propane can be significant, with fewer price swings and less routine delivery hassle. The coldest winter nights (design temp 12 °F) may require a backup heat strip or keeping your old furnace as emergency backup. But if your oil furnace is old or oil prices are up, the switch is increasingly sensible in Warwick—especially with utility rates easy to compare in PPL Electric territory. The numbers make less sense if your current system is only a few years old or you live in a leaky, drafty house that would trip up the best heat pump. Always run your own energy cost comparison.

Can I service my R-410A system after the refrigerant change?

Yes, you can continue to have your R-410A air conditioner or heat pump serviced in Warwick. The refrigerant phase-down only affects what is used in new systems—not what you already have. Contractors are still able to buy, handle, and top up R-410A when repairs are needed. The main thing to know is that as the supply shrinks over coming years, the price of R-410A is expected to climb, so fixing a major leak may eventually cost as much as putting in a new system. That doesn’t mean you need to rush out and replace a working unit. If servicing your existing system is practical, it’s absolutely legal and justified. Only consider replacement—and the cost of a new system at $5,200–$12,500 or more—when your repair bills start to approach the price of an upgrade.

What is commissioning and why does it matter?

Commissioning is the final check and startup tuning your contractor performs when an HVAC system is installed in Warwick. That means measuring airflow, refrigerant pressures, and temperature drops to make sure the system is operating exactly as designed by the manufacturer—not just plugged in and left running. If commissioning is skipped, even the best system can deliver poor comfort or high bills, especially in older homes or with existing ductwork that wasn't designed for today's equipment. You should see documentation of commissioning with any quality install bid, and you have every right to ask for the numbers. A system that isn’t properly commissioned can underperform and burn out faster, turning that investment of $8,200–$17,500 for a heat pump or AC into a short-lived headache. Good contractors include commissioning as standard protocol in Warwick and are happy to show you the readouts.

What HVAC tax credits are available to Warwick homeowners?

Warwick homeowners can access federal tax credits for qualifying high-efficiency heat pumps and central AC installations, as long as the equipment meets specified SEER2 and HSPF2 thresholds. Contractors in Warwick should be ready to document eligibility, but always check the IRS site for the latest terms. There aren’t specific Pennsylvania or Lancaster County tax credits for most residential HVAC upgrades—though special local rebates may apply through PPL Electric on qualifying equipment. The typical qualifying heat pump costs $8,200–$17,500 installed and can be eligible for hundreds of dollars in federal credits, which go through your tax return. Don’t base your decision on tax credits alone; make sure the equipment suits your home, climate, and current ductwork, as improper sizing or rushed retrofits aren’t worth a modest tax break. Always verify claims about eligibility with your own tax advisor, not just what’s promised in a bid.

Should I close vents in unused rooms?

In Warwick, it’s tempting to close vents in rooms you don’t use—especially with this area’s older homes and sometimes quirky duct runs. But closing vents typically doesn’t save energy. In most systems, restricting airflow throws off pressure balance and can force your furnace or AC to work against higher resistance. That means higher utility bills and premature wear, especially in homes with existing ductwork from before 1980 that likely isn’t sized for modulating airflow. It’s better to let your system “breathe” as designed and use a programmable thermostat to cut heating and cooling in unused spaces. If you regularly need to condition only part of your house, a ductless mini-split ($3,300–$7,200 installed for a single zone) is a better option. When in doubt, ask your HVAC pro to review the duct system before changing any airflow.

Who inspects HVAC work in Warwick?

In Warwick Township, as in most of Lancaster County, mechanical permits and inspections for HVAC installs or replacements are handled by the local township office. In Lancaster city, the city’s Bureau of Code Compliance takes care of this process. For things like filter changes or simple thermostat upgrades, you don’t need a permit or inspection here. For major jobs—like installing a new central system or adding ducts—an inspection ensures your installation meets the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), which is based on the IRC. Your contractor should pull the permit and arrange the inspection as part of their contract, not leave this for the homeowner to figure out. Expect to see the permit displayed at your property, and don’t pay in full upfront until the final sign-off. Typical replacement system costs in Warwick run $5,200–$12,500 depending on your setup and needs.

What does a heat pump cost installed in Warwick?

The installed price of a ducted heat pump system in Warwick typically ranges from $8,200–$17,500, with most standard replacements landing around $13,500. Costs vary based on your home’s square footage, insulation, whether you need electrical upgrades, and—crucially—whether your current ductwork is solid or needs work. In older homes or those without ducts, the only way to avoid the high end of the range is to use ductless mini-splits or high-velocity systems rather than brand-new ductwork. Don’t just pick by headline price: a quality installation costs more but pays off in reliability and comfort. If you get a quote significantly outside these ranges, make sure the scope and equipment are fully detailed before signing anything.

How cold does it get in Warwick and does that affect heat pumps?

Warwick winters hit design lows of about 12 °F, with 5,200 heating degree days a year. That means we live in a real winter climate and any new heat pump has to keep up when it counts. Modern cold-climate heat pumps function reliably at these temperatures and have backup electric heat strips for truly cold snaps, but older or entry-level models may not keep the house warm during a deep freeze. If you’re installing a new heat pump in Warwick, insist on one rated for low-ambient performance (9–11 HSPF2), and expect an installed range of $13,500–$17,500 for cold-climate models. Most residents with recent upgrades report excellent comfort year-round, especially in well-insulated homes, but your installer should always plan for the true local lows, not just a “mild winter” scenario.

Why do HVAC quotes in Warwick vary so much?

Quotes for HVAC work in Warwick often cover a wide range due to big differences in home age, ductwork quality, and even access for installation. Older homes (27.4% here built before 1980) can have hidden issues—undersized ducts, missing returns, or legacy wiring—that drive up installation cost. Some contractors cut corners by skipping load calculations, reusing questionable ductwork, or offering a “one size fits all” package with bare-minimum equipment, which is usually reflected in a lower price. Others include comprehensive commissioning, new linesets, or higher-end models with better HSPF2 or SEER2 ratings. For a new central AC, installed price may swing between $5,200–$12,500. Beware any quote that is dramatically lower or lacks detail—Warwick’s moderate suburban market is competitive, but you get what you pay for in both material and labor. Always ask for the breakdown of what’s included before you commit.
