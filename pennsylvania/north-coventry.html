Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, HVAC work comes with clear credentialing requirements. In most cases, HVAC contractors must hold a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration through the Pennsylvania Attorney General’s Office. If the project includes gas appliance work (like gas furnace installation or gas piping modifications), the installing technician must hold an active PA gas piping license. For air-conditioning and heat pump refrigerant handling, technicians must be certified under EPA Section 608—so ask to see the technician’s EPA 608 card before work begins.

Mechanical permits are required by most PA municipalities for HVAC replacement and for new installations. Like-for-like tasks such as thermostat swaps or filter changes typically do not need permits, but replacing major components or modifying ducting, wiring, or combustion-related parts usually does. For North Coventry specifically, verify what applies with the township or borough permitting office when your scope includes ductwork changes, new gas piping, or a full system replacement.

For incentives, check the PA Homeowner Rebate program and utility rebates (PPL Electric, PECO, Columbia Gas) for qualifying high-efficiency heat pumps and furnaces under Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) programs. ENERGY STAR certified equipment may also qualify for federal tax credits up to 30% under Section 25C.

To verify a contractor: (1) confirm PA HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov, (2) request an EPA 608 certificate from the technician, and (3) review certificate of insurance and workers’ compensation coverage documentation—ask for proof of coverage and confirm it matches the company name on the quote.

What is a SEER2 rating and why does it matter in North Coventry?

SEER2 is a modern cooling-efficiency rating for air conditioners and cooling modes of some heat pump systems, and it matters in North Coventry because you’ll use cooling during humid stretches of Chester County summers. Higher SEER2 generally means the unit uses less electricity to move the same amount of cooling, assuming the system is properly sized and installed. However, the real-world outcome also depends on airflow and duct performance—especially in North Coventry’s older housing stock, where many homes were built before 1980 (68.5% per U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). If ducts leak or are undersized for the actual airflow needs, the system can’t deliver performance as designed, and the efficiency advantage may disappear in daily operation. That’s why SEER2 should be considered alongside duct sealing, proper refrigerant charge procedures, thermostat calibration, and commissioning. When comparing options, look at the SEER2 range in the estimate and make sure the installer will perform the required startup and testing—not just install the equipment.
