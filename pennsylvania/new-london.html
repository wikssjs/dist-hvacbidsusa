Because about 19.1% of homes were built before 1980, it’s also typical to inspect older components and ensure modern refrigerant and control systems can be installed safely and efficiently.

Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, HVAC work is regulated to protect homeowners, and New London follows the same statewide rules enforced through local permitting. First, HVAC contractors generally must hold a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration through the Pennsylvania Attorney General’s Office. For gas appliances, the installing technician must hold an active PA gas piping license. For AC and heat pump refrigerant work, technicians must also have EPA Section 608 certification—ask to see the technician’s EPA 608 card.

Most PA municipalities require mechanical permits for HVAC replacement and new installation. A permit is typically not required for like-for-like changes such as thermostat swaps or filter changes, but you should still confirm with the township/borough where your home is located—especially if the job includes duct resizing, electrical modifications, or moving equipment. For rebates, check the PA Homeowner Rebate program and utility rebates (PPL Electric, PECO, Columbia Gas) for qualifying high-efficiency heat pumps and furnaces under Inflation Reduction Act-related programs. ENERGY STAR equipment may also qualify for federal tax credits up to 30% under Section 25C.

To verify a contractor for a New London project, start with the PA HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov, then request proof of EPA 608 certification from the technician assigned to your job. Also ask for a certificate of insurance and workers’ compensation documentation: confirm the dates and coverage limits are current, and verify the contractor’s policy lists the correct legal name. If they can’t provide certificates up front, pause before you sign.

Heat pump vs. furnace + AC — what makes sense in New London?

Pennsylvania’s four-season climate makes the heat pump vs. furnace decision more nuanced than in milder regions. New London experiences cold winter stretches, plus hot, humid summers that demand strong dehumidification—not just heating capacity. Modern cold-climate air-source heat pumps are designed to operate efficiently through much of the winter, and many Chester County systems can run effectively well beyond “mild weather” assumptions. Still, in an area where about 19.1% of homes were built before 1980, duct leakage, aging air handlers, and insulation gaps can reduce actual performance if the system is not matched to the home.

Here’s the practical part: if your home’s ducts are leaky or rooms are constantly uneven, ducted heat pump performance depends on fixing distribution. If you’re considering a switch to a ducted air-source heat pump, many installations land in the $7,500–$12,000 range, assuming your duct system can deliver. If the comparison is a traditional gas furnace + central AC setup, combined projects often land around $7,500–$12,000 for many New London cases—especially where a working duct system already exists.

A real-world justification example: if your ductwork is already in good shape and your gas line is established, a dual system approach can make sense when you want steady backup during very cold nights. But if you’re dealing with significant duct leakage or you’re also improving air sealing/insulation, the extra focus required for modern heat pump comfort may be money better spent upfront than maintaining two aging systems.

How to choose a HVAC installation contractor in New London

Choosing the right contractor in New London is mostly about verifying credentials, getting the scope right, and protecting yourself on payment and timelines. Start with licensing and certifications: confirm the contractor’s PA requirements using the HIC registration through the Pennsylvania Attorney General’s Office, then verify the technician credentials for the work you’re hiring for. For gas appliance installs, ask about the active PA gas piping license. For refrigerant handling in AC/heat pump projects, look for EPA Section 608 certification; reputable contractors can provide the technician’s card information before installation. If a contractor claims certifications but can’t show verification links or documents, that’s a red flag.

Next, request 2–3 itemized written quotes (not a single lump-sum). A good quote breaks down labor vs. materials, lists equipment model/efficiency (SEER2/HSPF2 or AFUE), and states exactly what is included—like duct sealing, electrical work, refrigerant line modifications, and any insulation updates. Pay attention to what’s excluded (demo, haul-away, permit labor, duct redesign, asbestos testing/abatement checks where applicable).

Warranty and payment matter. Insist on a workmanship warranty term plus separate equipment and materials warranties. For payment, never pay more than 10–15% upfront and use a holdback until the job is complete and tested. Finally, get arrival estimates and a completion window in writing so you’re not stuck waiting during Chester County’s busy season.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in New London

Do I need a permit to replace my HVAC in New London, PA?

In most Pennsylvania municipalities, a mechanical permit is required for HVAC replacement and new installation in New London. Like-for-like changes such as filter replacement or a thermostat swap typically don’t require a permit, but replacements that involve mechanical work—equipment swaps, new ducts/duct resizing, moving equipment, or electrical upgrades—often do. Because requirements can vary slightly by township/borough, a responsible contractor should confirm what’s required for your exact address before scheduling. You can also verify expectations with the township permitting office. When you’re evaluating bids, ask whether the contractor will pull the permit, handle inspection scheduling, and include that in the project timeline. If you’re installing a ducted heat pump or central AC replacement and the quote includes ductwork resizing, treat it as a permit-needed scope in most cases. Expect permit fees and plan review time to be modest, but they can still affect scheduling in Chester County’s busy season.

How do I choose a licensed HVAC contractor in New London?

Start by confirming the contractor’s Pennsylvania Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration through the Pennsylvania Attorney General’s office registry. Then verify the technician assigned to your job: for gas furnace work, the installing technician should have an active PA gas piping license, and for refrigerant handling on AC/heat pumps, you should ask to see EPA Section 608 certification. Next, require a certificate of insurance and workers’ compensation—don’t accept “we’re insured” without documents you can review. For the quote process, request 2–3 itemized written bids with labor and materials separated and equipment listed by make/model and efficiency. A good contractor will also explain how they’ll handle duct condition and whether additional work is anticipated. Finally, protect yourself with payment terms: never pay more than 10–15% upfront and keep a holdback until the system is tested. If one bid is dramatically lower—like a “central AC” quote that claims $6,000–$20,000 pricing without discussing ductwork—ask what’s excluded before you sign.

What is a SEER2 rating and why does it matter in New London?

SEER2 is the modern cooling efficiency metric used for air conditioners and many air-source heat pump cooling modes. In New London, where summers are typically hot and humid, SEER2 matters because it affects how much cooling your system delivers per unit of electricity—and it also relates to how well the equipment can run efficiently while maintaining comfort. Higher SEER2 equipment generally costs more upfront, but it can reduce operating costs over time. That said, the biggest comfort determinant is often whether your ductwork can deliver the airflow the system was designed for. In older Chester County housing stock—about 19.1% built before 1980—duct leakage and airflow restrictions can cause short cycling or uneven temperatures, which can reduce the real-world benefit of a higher SEER2 rating.

When you compare central AC options, look at the installed package scope, not just SEER2. A central AC replacement priced in the $6,000–$20,000 range may include duct sealing and airflow checks, while a lower-cost “equipment-only” install may leave leaky ducts untouched. Ask your contractor how they’ll verify airflow and whether they’ll include ductwork improvements needed to realize the efficiency you’re paying for.

How much does a new HVAC system cost in New London, PA?

Typical installed HVAC costs in New London depend on system type, ductwork condition, and how much electrical or distribution work is needed. For example, central AC replacement with existing ducts commonly falls into the $6,000–$20,000 band when the duct system is already workable. Ducted air-source heat pump installations often land in the $7,500–$12,000 range when ductwork and insulation support proper airflow and sizing. If you’re installing ductless mini-splits, the range can vary widely based on zones: a one-zone ductless project may land in the $7,500–$18,000 band, while multi-zone options can shift based on the number of indoor units and the complexity of line routing.

These are estimates for planning. Your final number can change when your contractor finds duct leakage, undersized returns, insufficient electrical capacity, or insulation gaps that require either better air sealing or updated sizing. Since New London is in Chester County—where projects frequently involve duct repair/resizing in older homes—always request an itemized quote that explains the scope, not just the equipment price.

Is a heat pump a good choice for New London's winters?

For many New London homeowners, a heat pump can be an excellent choice because modern cold-climate air-source heat pumps are engineered to maintain efficient heating through much of the winter. However, whether it’s a “slam dunk” depends on your home’s insulation, ductwork condition, and whether the system can deliver the required airflow. With about 19.1% of dwellings in the area built before 1980, duct leakage and insulation shortfalls can reduce performance if you install the equipment without addressing distribution and envelope issues.

A practical way to decide is to compare whole-system options. Ducted air-source heat pump installs often land in the $7,500–$12,000 range, and they can reduce heating operating cost compared with dual systems in many cases. If you have a lot of duct leakage or comfort issues, you may need duct sealing, sizing work, or a multi-zone approach. Some homes do well with dual fuel (heat pump plus gas backup), where the heat pump handles most heating and gas covers the coldest periods. Your contractor should base the decision on a load calculation and a real look at duct return pathways—not just outdoor temperature tables.

What HVAC tax credits and rebates are available for New London homeowners?

New London homeowners may have access to both federal and state/utility incentives when installing qualifying high-efficiency equipment. At the federal level, ENERGY STAR certified systems may qualify for a federal tax credit up to 30% under Section 25C, assuming the equipment meets eligibility requirements for your application. For many air-source heat pump installations, the opportunity commonly discussed is a 30% tax credit + utility rebates, but eligibility rules can vary by product and scenario. On the state and utility side, check the PA Homeowner Rebate program and utility rebates through providers serving Chester County such as PPL Electric, PECO, and Columbia Gas for qualifying heat pumps and furnaces.

Because qualification depends on equipment efficiency ratings and the type of system installed, ask your contractor to provide the equipment specifications (SEER2/HSPF2 for heat pumps or AFUE for furnaces) so you can confirm eligibility before scheduling. Also plan for timing: rebates and credits often require proof of installation and sometimes specific documentation forms submitted after the job is complete.
