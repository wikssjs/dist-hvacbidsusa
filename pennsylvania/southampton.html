Southampton heating and cooling prices by system type — 2026

Southampton stands out for its high homeownership rate and older housing stock, with 84.2% of homes owner-occupied and 43.3% built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). This means most local projects are replacements or retrofits, not new installations. Much of Franklin County’s housing follows the 1950–1980 ranch or split-level style, often with ductwork that was added for heating alone—only occasionally sized for modern central air or heat pump systems. Duct upgrades are common, especially when replacing equipment that first served a heating-only system.

With 5,200 heating degree days and only 1,250 cooling degree days annually, Southampton has a definite heating-dominated climate. That skews system selection: homeowners here benefit more from performance and efficiency gains in heating rather than cooling. The bulk of utility costs are spent staying warm through cold winters, so a high-efficiency furnace or heat pump will pay back more quickly than an ultra-high-SEER air conditioner.

What construction era means for your system

In Southampton, a big chunk of the housing was built from 1950 to 1980, in the era of ranch and split-level homes. Back then, central air was often an afterthought—ducts were sized for oil or gas furnaces, and cooling loads were small. If your home falls into that pre-1980 group (43.3%), there’s a good chance your existing ducts are undersized for today’s higher airflow requirements, especially if you're swapping a furnace for a ducted heat pump or higher-SEER air conditioner. Duct leakage and poor insulation were also common in construction of that era.

Older windows and less insulation mean higher heating and cooling demand compared to modern homes, directly affecting system sizing and efficiency. Chimneys and flues may show age, which matters if you intend to stick with a gas furnace or upgrade venting. If you’re considering an all-electric system, check your main panel capacity—many older homes only have 100-amp service, sometimes too tight for a heat pump plus other major appliances.

Homes in newer developments, typically on natural gas in places like Chambersburg, generally have serviceable ductwork and adequate electric panels—but even those houses are now facing their first major replacement, as original equipment hits its limit around the 20–25 year mark. Whether your house has solid “bones” or needs basic upgrades, Southampton’s housing wave means you need an honest look at duct condition and insulation before sinking money into premium equipment.

What each system actually costs to run in Southampton

When figuring out what you’ll actually spend month-to-month on heating or cooling in Southampton, you need three numbers: how efficient your system is, how much heating and cooling our local weather demands, and what you pay per unit of fuel or electricity. For reference, West Penn Power is the main electric utility for most Franklin County residents.

Efficiency matters—an 8 HSPF2 heat pump uses less electricity per BTU than a lower-rated model. Annual energy needed depends on climate; Southampton sees roughly 5,200 heating degree days and 1,250 cooling degree days per year, so heating drives the bill.

Here’s how the math works, using example figures:

  • Suppose your house needs 50 million BTUs (MMBTU) of heating in a year. That’s typical for Southampton’s older housing in a heating season.
  • Your heat pump has a rating of 9 HSPF2 (means it delivers 9 BTUs per watt-hour). To deliver 50 MMBTU, you’ll use about 5,560 kWh per winter.
  • Multiply by your actual electric rate (find it on your West Penn Power bill)—if it’s $0.14/kWh, that’s $778 a season.

If heating with gas or oil, factor in efficiency (AFUE) and fuel cost. But these numbers move a lot based on your house’s insulation, window condition, and—very important—duct leakage. Leaky ducts can waste 20–30% of your heating energy, making even a high-efficiency system disappoint.

Bottom line: upgrade the system, but don’t cheap out on ducts or ignore insulation, or your utility bill won’t budge much.

R-410A phase-down: repair or replace?

If your existing central air or heat pump in Southampton runs on R-410A, service remains legal and available. The big change is for new equipment—federal rules are phasing down R-410A in favor of lower-GWP refrigerants like R-32 and R-454B. This is about environmental goals, but it also impacts prices of R-410A as supply tightens over the next few years.

What does this mean for you? If your system is working well and not leaking, keep using it. Nothing about the rules makes your R-410A equipment obsolete or unserviceable. Contractors can continue to repair and recharge these systems as long as R-410A supplies last, which will be years, not months.

The real shift? The cost of R-410A is rising. On an older system with a slow refrigerant leak, a recharge today costs more than it did a few years ago—and will continue creeping up. If your system develops a major leak and is near end of life, the higher refrigerant cost tips the scales toward replacement, especially if you’re facing a big bill for both refrigerant and labor. But as long as your system works and doesn’t leak, you’re not forced to upgrade by code or by law.

What size system does a Southampton home need?

Right-sizing your equipment is the most critical decision when installing or replacing HVAC in Southampton. Oversizing—putting in too large a system—remains the contractor mistake responsible for high utility bills, muggy summers, uneven heating and cooling, and short equipment life. It isn’t a margin of safety, it’s a flaw.

Reputable contractors should run a Manual J load calculation. This method takes into account your actual square footage, insulation levels, window types, orientation, and the winter design temperature (12 °F for this area). The calculation determines exactly how many BTUs your home loses at the coldest expected temperature, sizing the system to meet—not overshoot—this load. Don’t accept rules of thumb like “one ton per 500 square feet,” as they ignore older construction and air leaks common in Southampton’s pre-1980 homes.

The right question to ask: “Did you run a load calculation, and can I see it?” If your contractor only matches to your old unit’s size or skips the calculation, they're repeating mistakes made decades ago, often when insulation and windows were worse than today. Sizing is too important to leave to guessing.

SEER2, HSPF2 and AFUE decoded

SEER2 measures a cooling system’s average seasonal efficiency—higher SEER2 means lower summer electricity bills. HSPF2 rates heat pumps for winter efficiency, and AFUE is for furnace efficiency, telling you how much of the fuel turns into usable heat. These “2” versions are updated, stricter tests; don’t compare old SEER/HSPF numbers directly to SEER2/HSPF2.

Franklin County, with its 5,200 heating degree days and just 1,250 cooling degree days, is a heating-dominated market. That means HSPF2 and AFUE ratings matter much more than chasing the highest SEER2 score. Paying extra for a top-tier SEER2 system is rarely a smart investment here—the cooling season is short, so annual savings are modest. For most Southampton homes, a SEER2 rating around 16–18 is enough, but look for higher HSPF2 if you’re relying on a heat pump for primary heating. For gas heat, an AFUE of 95% is already very efficient, and jumping to the highest available often isn’t worth the price jump. Match your investment to our climate—not someone else’s.

Permits, licensing and inspections in Southampton

In Pennsylvania, there’s no statewide HVAC trade license—residential work is regulated at the local level. For Southampton, that means permitting is handled by each township or borough; in Chambersburg, for example, central HVAC replacements require a mechanical permit. Elsewhere in Franklin County, check with your local office to see what’s necessary.

Contractors must have a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration if your job is over $500. You can—and should—verify this registration at the state’s official website. The Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act also requires a written contract for work above that amount and gives you three business days to cancel after signing.

Anyone handling refrigerant needs federal EPA Section 608 certification—no exceptions—so always ask. As for insurance, workers’ compensation is mandatory for every HVAC employer in Pennsylvania. You can check coverage online through the actual WCAIS system named in the rules. Permits, proper certification, and insurance protect you if something goes wrong—and any contractor who balks at showing proof isn’t worth your risk.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in Southampton

How often should I change my filter? For most Southampton homes, changing your HVAC filter every 1–3 months is the right schedule, but it depends on your filter type, pets, local dust, and whether you're running the system heavily for heating. Many homes here are older (43.3% built before 1980), which can mean dustier ducts, so a good rule is to check monthly—if it looks dirty, swap it out. Remember, a clogged filter can reduce airflow, lowering efficiency and leading to expensive repairs. If you keep up with this simple habit, your equipment will perform better and you’ll maximize your investment—essential if you’ve spent $4,900–$12,000 on a new central air system or $7,900–$17,000 on a heat pump.
Are duct sealing and insulation eligible for incentives? Duct sealing and insulation improvements are often eligible for utility or government efficiency incentives, but the availability changes year to year. In Southampton (Franklin County), local programs sometimes partner with Pennsylvania’s larger efficiency efforts, which can help curb the real losses: older homes, often dating to the 1950–1980 wave, can lose 20–30% of heated or cooled air through duct leaks. When you’re weighing a new system—whether it’s a $13,000–$17,000 cold-climate heat pump or an upgraded AC—tackling ducts can deliver more bang for your buck. Always check your utility’s website (West Penn Power, for example) and state energy programs before you sign a contract. Your contractor should know which incentives are currently active and help document eligible improvements.
How cold does it get in Southampton and does that affect heat pumps? Southampton’s winter design temperature is 12 °F, which means heating systems must handle stretches at or below that number. Older single-stage heat pumps often struggled at these temps, but newer cold-climate heat pumps are built for exactly this range. If your home is typical of Franklin County's pre-1980 stock—with decent insulation and no huge drafts—a cold-climate ducted heat pump (costing $13,000–$17,000 installed) can handle most of your heating, sometimes without backup. The short answer: yes, it gets cold, but manufacturers now engineer heat pumps that perform reliably in our climate. Ask your contractor about the specific cold-weather performance for the models they offer.
How much does a new HVAC system cost in Southampton? The cost depends on what you're installing and what your house needs. For a straightforward central AC replacement using existing ducts, expect to spend $4,900–$12,000. If you’re adding a ducted air-source heat pump (often recommended in our heating-heavy climate), the installed price lands between $7,900–$17,000. Full duct system installations—needed in many Franklin County homes built before widespread air conditioning—can run $8,900–$18,000 before you even add equipment. Always budget for possible ductwork upgrades—older Southampton houses often need it.
When is the best time of year to replace HVAC in Southampton? The best timing is usually spring or fall—what contractors call “shoulder seasons”—when systems aren’t under heavy use and contractors aren’t booked solid with emergencies. In Southampton, this usually means March–May or September–October. Scheduling during these windows can get you faster installation and sometimes off-season pricing on equipment, which is important since a full system could run from $7,900–$17,000. Planning ahead also gives time for permits (which are required for most jobs here) and allows for any needed duct upgrades, which are common in the older ranch or split-level homes in Franklin County.
Does Pennsylvania license HVAC contractors? Pennsylvania does not issue a statewide trade license specifically for HVAC contractors. In Southampton, you should look for a contractor with Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for work over $500, which is managed by the state. Mechanical permits and inspections are handled at the township or borough level—for example, the Borough of Chambersburg has specific permit requirements for HVAC projects, but rules differ elsewhere. Always confirm your contractor has current HIC registration and federal EPA Section 608 certification for refrigerant handling. For a project costing anywhere from $4,900–$17,000, these credentials matter.
What does a heat pump cost installed in Southampton? Installing a new air-source (ducted) heat pump in a typical Southampton home costs between $7,900–$17,000, depending on the size of your home, ductwork condition, and the efficiency rating you choose. Cold-climate-rated heat pumps, which are built to handle our 12 °F winter design temperature, push toward the high end of that range—but in exchange give you consistent heat through the winter. Ductless mini-splits for single rooms or additions cost less, between $3,200–$6,900 per zone. Get a written, line-item estimate that spells out equipment, labor and any duct or electrical upgrades needed.
What MERV filter rating should I use? For most Southampton homes, a MERV 8 or MERV 11 filter strikes the best balance—enough to capture dust, pollen and standard allergens, but still allow enough air through your older (often undersized) ductwork. Using a high-MERV filter (like MERV 13) in a unit not designed for it can starve airflow, straining equipment (especially if you’ve just spent $4,900–$12,000 on a new system). If you or someone in your house has severe allergies, check if your blower and duct system can handle a higher grade—your HVAC contractor can tell you during a maintenance visit or replacement consultation. Always check your filter monthly in heating season; local homes built before 1980 can surprise you with how much dust the system pulls in.
Can a heat pump heat my house without backup? In Southampton’s cold winters, modern cold-climate heat pumps often can heat well without backup—especially in homes with upgraded insulation and tight ductwork. Older heat pumps (and some builder-grade units still found in Franklin County) tended to need electric resistance coils, propane, or oil backup when outside temps dropped toward that 12 °F design mark. Now, many ducted models labeled “cold-climate” will handle all but the absolute coldest stretches, although sizing and duct condition matter. Expect to pay $13,000–$17,000 for a cold-climate ducted heat pump installed. Always get the manufacturer’s published temperature specs and ensure your contractor did a Manual J heat loss calculation before you commit—don’t take “it should be fine” from anyone.
