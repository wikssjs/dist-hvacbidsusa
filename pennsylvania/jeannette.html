The refrigerant change and what it means for repairs

A change is underway in what refrigerants are used in new heating and cooling systems. Newly manufactured residential systems in Jeannette—like everywhere—are now being built for lower-GWP refrigerants such as R-454B and R-32, thanks to a national phase-down of older, higher-GWP HFCs. However, if your current system uses R-410A (the most common refrigerant since the mid-2000s), it’s still legal to service, repair, and refill. No one will force you to replace a working system just because of this change.

The real impact lands when your older system springs a refrigerant leak. R-410A is still available, but over the next few years, its supply will tighten, driving up prices on recharges and repairs. This doesn’t make your equipment obsolete overnight, but it does shift the calculus: major repairs—especially for leaks—will cost more and may make replacement a better investment on middle-aged systems.

If your system is in good shape and not leaking, you don’t need to do anything; there’s no requirement for a retrofit here in Jeannette. But if you’re facing repeated refrigerant issues, or if your unit is genuinely due for replacement, expect your new equipment to use one of the newer, lower-impact refrigerants. These offer similar performance and safety, and every contractor working on refrigerant is required by federal law to carry EPA Section 608 certification.

Modern cold-climate heat pumps are now a serious option well below freezing—capacity loss isn’t what it was a decade ago, and most good systems keep delivering heat even in single digits. Still, as temperature drops, heat pump output dips, so in serious cold snaps, some homes rely on electric resistance strips or a backup furnace—hence the value of dual-fuel systems.

Natural gas is widely available throughout Westmoreland County, but not universal—some rural areas are still on propane or oil, making heat pumps the sensible alternative there. When gas is at the curb, a standard gas furnace remains the lowest upfront cost and most reliable deep-winter performer. In places where gas isn’t available, heat pumps are usually more cost-effective and lower maintenance than oil or propane—especially with rising fuel costs and the push for electrification.

If your house is particularly drafty, oversized, or prone to losing power, gas or dual-fuel may still fit best. For well-sealed, average-sized homes, even in Jeannette’s winter, today’s high-efficiency heat pumps can do the heavy lifting.

Running the numbers on Jeannette's climate

To understand what your heating or cooling system will really cost to run in Jeannette, it’s worth running a simple calculation. Here’s the process: Take your system’s efficiency rating—AFUE for gas furnaces; HSPF2 for heat pumps; SEER2 for cooling. Next, multiply your annual heating or cooling degree days—5,600 for heating, 1,050 for cooling in Jeannette—by your home’s thermal load (in BTUs), to estimate annual energy use.

For example, suppose your electric bill from West Penn Power shows you pay 14 cents/kWh. If a heat pump delivers 9 HSPF2, that means for every kilowatt-hour it uses, it provides about 3 times that in heat. To get a rough idea of annual cost, you take the degree day load, estimate your home’s heating demand, and divide by system efficiency. For gas, compare the fuel cost and AFUE percentage.

But, real-world costs swing on two wild cards: your house and your ducts. A high-efficiency furnace or heat pump on old, leaky ductwork will underperform a mid-grade system on tight, well-sealed ducts. If your ductwork loses 25% of its heat (not uncommon in older Jeannette homes), you’re leaving money on the table regardless of what you paid for the equipment. Always factor duct condition into those calculator numbers on contractor websites; they’re only as good as your weakest link.

Mechanical permits for HVAC work in Jeannette

Pennsylvania does not issue a specific statewide HVAC contractor license for residential work. Instead, contractors must be registered as Home Improvement Contractors—registration is required for any job over $500, which covers nearly all HVAC installations and replacements, and you can verify a contractor’s registration directly online. Mechanical work, including installing or replacing furnaces, heat pumps, ductwork, or central air, is regulated at the municipal level in Westmoreland County. In Jeannette, mechanical permits are processed by local township or city offices and nearly always require an inspection after installation to ensure compliance with the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code.

Every technician handling refrigerant—whether installing, charging, or recovering—must carry a federal EPA Section 608 certification, regardless of state or local registration. Insurance and workers' compensation are mandatory for all employers in Pennsylvania and can be confirmed using the online verification system. When you hire, ask your contractor for proof of both Home Improvement Contractor registration and up-to-date workers’ compensation; this protects both you and the crew on your property.

When ductless is the right answer

Ductless mini-splits really shine when you’re heating and cooling a space where ductwork isn’t already in place or would be expensive or disruptive to add. For Jeannette’s housing—often built before central air was standard—it’s common to face rooms, additions, or entire upper stories that never got proper duct runs. Ductless solves that with minimal construction mess, no lost closets or soffits, and targeted comfort where it’s needed.

Single-zone systems (one outdoor, one indoor head) are the clear choice for isolated spaces: an addition, a finished attic, a sunroom. Multi-zone setups (one outdoor unit with several indoor heads) cover multiple rooms, but they can be less efficient than several single-zone units because of load imbalance; one indoor head running alone may be oversized for the load, leading to cycling and less comfort.

Head placement matters—aesthetics are in the eye of the beholder, but for most Jeannette homes with mid-century trim or original plaster, the tradeoff is between a discreet wall-mounted unit and the cost or mess of new ductwork. Don’t overlook condensate lines—every indoor head needs a reliable way to drain water, and sloppy installations are a red flag. In this market, where duct and electrical upgrades are routine, ductless is often the least invasive and most cost-effective answer, whether for targeted zones or whole-home retrofits.

How to vet an HVAC contractor in Jeannette

The most important decision you make isn’t about brand, it’s about the quality of installation. In Jeannette and the broader Westmoreland County area, two crews can put in the same equipment—one by the book, one cutting corners—and deliver wildly different results. The nicest system will underperform if it’s not sized, installed, and set up properly.

Start your vetting by checking the Pennsylvania Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registry to verify legal status for jobs over $500. Make sure the technician physically handling refrigerant is EPA Section 608 certified—it’s non-negotiable. Ask if a Manual J load calculation will be performed—not just square footage—and look for equipment model numbers (not just “3-ton heat pump”) clearly listed on your bid. Commissioning is often skipped; insist on documentation of startup airflow and refrigerant charge readings.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in Jeannette

How much does ductwork add to an HVAC installation?

For a typical Jeannette home that was built before central air was standard, installing a full duct system can be a significant portion of your project cost. Adding new ducts to an existing house is usually in the $8,100–$16,000 range, depending on house size, layout, and whether you’re doing full main trunk lines or just adding lines to a few rooms. Many mid-20th-century homes in Jeannette require creative routing and minor structural changes to hide new ducts, and attic or basement retrofits can run toward the high end of the range. If your existing ducts are sound and just need minor sealing or repairs, your cost will be far lower. Always ask your contractor to inspect your current ductwork before quoting a new full-system install.

How much does a new HVAC system cost in Jeannette?

A new HVAC system in Jeannette depends a lot on what’s already in your home and whether you have working ductwork. Most homeowners replacing a central AC (using existing ducts) pay between $4,500–$11,000. A high-efficiency ducted heat pump replacement runs $7,200–$15,500. If your home never had central air, or ductwork is beyond salvaging, a full duct system plus equipment can total $8,100–$16,000. Ductless mini-splits are much less for a single room or zone, typically $2,900–$6,300. Always get a professional inspection; older Jeannette homes often need electrical or duct upgrades that move the price up.

What is SEER2 and how is it different from SEER?

SEER2 is the latest efficiency rating for air conditioning and heat pumps. It measures how much cooling a system delivers per unit of electricity, but the updated test (SEER2) uses higher static pressure to better reflect real-world duct conditions—something very relevant to Jeannette’s older homes, where ducts may be original or undersized. SEER2 numbers are slightly lower than old SEER ratings for the same equipment, but are more honest about what you’ll actually experience after installation. For example, a new central AC rated at 16–20 SEER2 ($4,500–$11,000) will generally outperform an old 13 SEER unit, especially when paired with properly sealed ducts.

How often should I change my filter?

Most Jeannette homes with forced-air furnaces or heat pumps need their filters replaced every 1–3 months, especially during heavy heating or cooling seasons. If your home was built before 1980 and has upgraded ductwork, a high-performance filter may gather more dust—check it monthly. Construction dust or pets mean more frequent changes. A clogged filter restricts airflow, reducing efficiency and potentially causing expensive problems, whether your system cost $4,500–$11,000 or twice that. Always use the filter size recommended by your contractor, and avoid “overly restrictive” after-market filters unless your blower is sized for them.

What HVAC tax credits are available to Jeannette homeowners?

Jeannette homeowners can take advantage of federal tax credits for qualifying high-efficiency heating and cooling upgrades, including heat pumps and some central AC/furnace combinations. For instance, a cold-climate heat pump that costs $11,500–$15,500 often qualifies for credits and utility rebates, especially if it meets or exceeds ENERGY STAR standards. West Penn Power customers may also see additional rebates, though amounts and eligibility can change year to year. Always consult your contractor for current incentive details and ensure any paperwork is completed for you—they'll need to know the equipment model numbers, efficiency ratings, and installation date to qualify.

Does Pennsylvania license HVAC contractors?

Pennsylvania does not issue a specific HVAC license at the state level; instead, contractors working on residential jobs valued over $500 must register as Home Improvement Contractors. This is governed under Pennsylvania law and enforced in Jeannette and across Westmoreland County. It’s essential to check that your contractor is properly registered; you can do this at the official state registry. For anything involving refrigerant, the technician is also federally required to have EPA Section 608 certification. For most homes—whether you're spending $2,900–$6,300 on a mini-split or more on a new system—always confirm insurance and registration before signing.

How cold does it get in Jeannette and does that affect heat pumps?

Jeannette’s winter design temperature is 8 °F, with 5,600 heating degree days—a climate where reliable heating matters. Older heat pumps would struggle in this cold, but modern cold-climate models keep working well below freezing, though their output drops at the coldest temps. If your home has strong insulation and good ductwork, a heat pump in the $7,200–$15,500 band can cover nearly all your heat, sometimes backed up by electric strips or a gas furnace for deep cold. In rural townships without natural gas, heat pumps often outperform propane or oil. A trusted contractor will size your system for local conditions, not just “national averages.”

Do I need to be home during installation?

For most Jeannette installations, it’s best for someone to be home for at least part of the day—especially when crews arrive and before they wrap up. The contractor will need access inside for thermostat, vents, duct inspection, and system startup. Depending on the job—if you’re getting a ductless mini-split ($2,900–$6,300), or a full system—expect 1–3 days on site. You don’t have to take off work the whole time, but do plan to be available for any questions, walkthroughs, and final commissioning. Always confirm access and expectations with your contractor in advance, so there are no surprises.

Should I replace the furnace and AC at the same time?

Many Jeannette homes run a matched furnace and air conditioner. If one side fails and the other is more than 10–15 years old, replacing both often makes sense—especially with incentives for efficiency upgrades and when ductwork or electrical requires upgrades anyway. Coordinating a full swap typically reduces labor cost and downtime over two separate jobs. If your existing furnace is recent and efficient, and the AC just died, it’s fine to replace just the AC ($4,500–$11,000). But mismatched systems can reduce comfort and may lose out on rebates. Have a pro evaluate your system age and duct condition to make the call.
