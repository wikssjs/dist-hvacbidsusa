Operating cost, not sticker price

The sticker price of a new HVAC unit only tells half the story. In a climate like Tredyffrin’s—with 4,800 heating degree days and a winter design temperature of 14 °F—your ongoing utility bills matter even more. Here’s how you actually compare operating costs:

First, efficiency ratings (like SEER2 for cooling or HSPF2 for heat pumps) tell you how much energy your unit uses to produce heating or cooling. Next, you take Chester County’s climate data—the number of degree days—to estimate how much heating or cooling your house will need in a typical year. The bigger the gap between outdoor and indoor temperatures, the more your system runs.

Finally, multiply the expected kilowatt-hours (for electric heat pumps or AC) or therms (for gas furnaces) by your actual utility rate. In Tredyffrin, PECO Energy provides both electric and natural gas service in most neighborhoods. You’ll find your specific $/kWh or $/therm clearly listed on your utility bill.

For example, let’s say a 3-ton heat pump with 9 HSPF2 is rated to deliver 24,000 BTUs per hour. If your home needs 32,000,000 BTUs over a winter (using the 4,800 heating degree days and a sizing calculation), you’d divide annual load by efficiency to estimate the kWh usage. Then multiply by your PECO rate.

But here’s the catch: if your ductwork leaks, is poorly insulated, or sized incorrectly, even the most efficient unit wastes energy. Duct leakage can easily cancel out the gains from a more efficient system. Don’t upgrade equipment without addressing the ducts—you’ll pay for the sticker, but not get the savings.

What a Pennsylvania HVAC contractor must hold

Pennsylvania does not issue a statewide HVAC trade license for residential jobs. Instead, HVAC contractors must hold a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for projects over $500. This is managed at the state level by the Office of Attorney General—but enforcement, and any extra licensing beyond this, is handled locally by each municipality. In Chester County, towns like Tredyffrin and West Chester run their own mechanical permit programs when you install or replace central HVAC systems.

No matter what, any technician working with refrigerant must have EPA Section 608 certification. This is a federal requirement and applies to all contractors, regardless of local rules.

Permits for HVAC replacement or new installation are usually handled by the code office in your specific municipality. Inspections focus on energy code compliance and safety.

You should verify a contractor’s HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov before you sign a contract. For insurance, ask for their certificate of insurance and check workers’ compensation status via the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov). Never work with anyone who can’t provide these verifications up front, regardless of referral or reputation.

Maintenance that actually extends system life

Most HVAC service contracts bundle in things you could do yourself, then up-sell “tune-ups” you might not need. Here’s what actually pays off for Tredyffrin systems:

First: change your filter regularly, using the right MERV rating. Too restrictive a filter can choke airflow in older systems, so don’t just buy the highest MERV you see—check your blower’s specs. For most homes, every 2–3 months is right, but check monthly in seasons of heavy use.

Second: ensure annual coil cleaning and that the condensate line flows freely. Dirt buildup on indoor or outdoor coils lowers efficiency—and in our humid summers, a clogged condensate drain can mean water backups.

Third: check refrigerant charge. Don’t let anyone top up “just because.” A system that’s low has a leak, and adding refrigerant without fixing the leak is not maintenance—it’s papering over a real problem.

Service plans are worth it only if they actually include priority scheduling, verified cleaning, and a documented check—not just a filter swap. Avoid paying for “plans” that only add up to a phone reminder. Schedule maintenance in early spring or early fall, ahead of the peak heating and cooling rush in Tredyffrin, so you aren’t stuck waiting during emergencies.

Manual J and the tonnage question

If a contractor gets one thing wrong on your Tredyffrin HVAC install, it’s usually sizing. Oversized systems short-cycle, fail to dehumidify in our humid summers, and wear out early. An oversized unit is not “safer” or “better”—it just means more breakdown calls and uncomfortable rooms.

The right way to size is by running a Manual J load calculation, which accounts for wall construction, windows, insulation, orientation, and those critical climate numbers—like our winter design temperature of 14 °F. This calculates the actual BTU demand so the system matches your house, not a rule of thumb.

Simply swapping in whatever size is on your old nameplate just forwards someone else’s mistake. And square-footage rules (like 1 ton per 500 sq ft) aren’t good enough for Chester County’s housing stock, where insulation and envelope tightness can vary a lot even in homes built the same year.

The question to ask any bidder: “Did you do a Manual J load calculation, and can I see it?” A real pro will show their work. Don’t settle for guesswork—it’s the difference between comfort (and lower energy bills) and years of frustration.
