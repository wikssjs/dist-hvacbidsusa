What a real HVAC quote contains

Not all HVAC quotes are meaningful for comparison. In Moon, a detailed quote should break down both the equipment and the work itself. Insist on model numbers for both indoor and outdoor units—a mismatched pair may not deliver its advertised efficiency or comfort, and can affect warranties. The quote should state the system tonnage and attach a load calculation, not just pick the size of your old equipment. It must cover ductwork inspection, with details on sealing or modifications required. Any line set replacement or documented reuse should be included. Electrical work, especially in older homes, must be specific: will they upgrade the breaker or the panel if needed?

You should see a section on the condensate drain, new or existing thermostat, and confirmation that the municipal permit cost is included if required. Once installed, a proper commissioning with documented airflow and refrigerant charge—called a startup report—proves the install was done right. Finally, clarify the labor warranty, since it's different from the manufacturer's equipment warranty.

A quote that names only a brand and a price is not really a quote—it's a placeholder and impossible to compare meaningfully.

The refrigerant change and what it means for repairs

Most new residential HVAC systems installed in 2025 and beyond use lower-GWP refrigerants like R-454B or R-32, instead of the old standard R-410A. This has nothing to do with immediate safety or legality; it's about federal requirements to phase down higher-impact greenhouse gases. If you have a working R-410A system in Moon, nothing makes it illegal or even unserviceable. Service technicians can and do add R-410A to existing systems, and plenty stock it for repairs. However, the reality is that as these refrigerants become less common, supply tightens, and prices climb. So, if you spring a refrigerant leak several years from now, topping up or repairing that older R-410A system will likely be costlier than it is today.

This factors into repair-versus-replacement decisions mostly for older units nearing their normal service life or those that need major refrigerant-related repairs. Repairing a significant refrigerant leak, especially if it involves replacing a coil or compressor, might tip the balance toward replacement when the labor and refrigerant cost stacks up. Bottom line: keep your R-410A system running if it’s otherwise healthy, but know that when big refrigerant repairs crop up after a decade or more, the economics look different than they did five years ago.

Repair or replace your Moon system?

Deciding to repair or replace your HVAC in Moon comes down to a few clear factors. First, consider age: if your system is under 10 years old and suffers a single component failure, repair is almost always your best move. Once you’re into the 10–15 year range, weigh the repair cost against a new system. If one repair is more than a third of a new unit, especially if it involves coils or circuit boards, start considering replacement—these costs will not reverse with age.

Efficiency is a factor but don't overestimate its impact. In a heating-dominated market like Moon, a super-efficient system only pays back if your ductwork and home envelope are tight—and many in pre-1980 homes are not. A system with R-22 refrigerant absolutely should be replaced if it develops a leak, but R-410A systems remain legal to service (see above). The two non-negotiable cases are a cracked heat exchanger or a compressor failure on an old system. These carry safety or cost thresholds where replacement is required, not optional.

Permits, licensing and inspections in Moon

In Pennsylvania, there’s no statewide license for HVAC contractors; instead, residential HVAC is regulated locally. The Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), based on the International Residential Code (IRC), is enforced by either your municipality or, if they opt out, by the PA Department of Labor & Industry. In Allegheny County, permits for new installations, replacements, or major alterations are required in the City of Pittsburgh, but Moon itself may handle permitting differently depending on municipal enforcement.

To legally contract for HVAC work over $500, contractors must register as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. Always verify this registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Workers’ compensation insurance is mandatory for any employer, and you can check a contractor’s status via the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov) before hiring. Beyond the state and local rules, any tech handling refrigerants must hold EPA Section 608 certification—a federal requirement, so don’t ignore it.

Permits and inspections for residential HVAC are almost always required for major installs or replacements. You should see permit fees and processes spelled out directly in your contractor’s quote or contract. Never skip them; doing so can create issues during home sales or insurance claims.

Humidity and air quality in Moon homes

With long, cold winters and a decent share of heating degree days, dryness becomes a problem for most Moon homes. In contrast, warm and humid spells hit in summer, but not for as long as in southern states. That split means you might need a whole-house humidifier for comfort in January but a properly sized and installed AC to manage stickiness in July. Avoid oversized AC units—if your system’s too big, it’ll cool but fail to dehumidify, making summer air feel clammy.

For older homes, be careful with adding higher MERV air filters. High filtration adds resistance that not every old blower can handle. For most Moon homes, a MERV 8–11 strikes a reasonable balance between particle capture and system airflow. As more homes are air-sealed and insulated, mechanical ventilation (ERV/HRV) becomes increasingly useful, but these systems aren’t necessary in most leaky, older houses common north of the river.

Add-ons—UV lights, room ionizers, very high-MERV filters—are often oversold for typical Moon air quality concerns. They can be maintenance headaches for limited real-world gain. Focus first on duct sealing, appropriate filter upgrades, and keeping your humidifier clean and serviced.

Running the numbers on Moon's climate

Moon’s climate is heating-driven, so any payback math for new HVAC focuses on heating efficiency. With 5,600 heating degree days (HDD) and only 1,050 cooling degree days (CDD), almost all your energy dollars go to heat. The local electric utility is Duquesne Light Company, and your electricity rate is listed right on your bill. Here’s how to think it through:

Suppose you’re deciding between a standard (8 HSPF2) and a high-efficiency (10 HSPF2) heat pump. Say your house needs 40 million BTUs of heat per year (figure based on size, insulation, and degree days). First, divide the load by the HSPF2 rating to estimate energy used:

  • Standard model: 40,000,000 BTUs ÷ 8 HSPF2 = 5,000 kWh per year
  • High efficiency: 40,000,000 BTUs ÷ 10 HSPF2 = 4,000 kWh per year

At this point, check your Duquesne Light bill for your “per kWh” rate. Multiply by 1,000-kWh increments for yearly heating cost. The difference (about 1,000 kWh/year in this example) is what the higher-efficiency system saves you yearly. If your electric rate is $0.13 per kWh, that’s $130 saved per year. Compare that to the installed price difference—typically $8,600–$18,000 for heat pumps here—and you can figure a simple payback timeline.

But there’s a big catch: leaky ductwork or poor insulation can erase these gains. A high-efficiency system on bad ducts won’t outperform a middle-tier system on well-sealed ones. Building envelope and duct condition matter just as much as equipment ratings for savings in the real world.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in Moon

Do I need dual fuel in southwestern Pennsylvania? Dual fuel systems—where a heat pump is paired with a gas furnace—are sometimes recommended in climates with cold winters and shoulder seasons. In Moon, with 5,600 heating degree days, winters are long and design temps can hit 8 °F. Dual fuel makes sense if you want the cost savings of electric heat for milder weather but need reliable warmth on the coldest nights, when heat pump efficiency drops. However, most Moon homes with natural gas (which is widely available) can use a high-efficiency furnace as the main heat without the complexity. Consider dual fuel if your electricity is cheaper than gas or if you already have a newer furnace and only need to add a heat pump ($8,600–$18,000). Otherwise, weigh install costs against real savings; in many cases, a single-fuel system is simpler and just as effective for our winters.
Do I need to be home during installation? While it’s not strictly required to be home for the entire installation, it’s wise for at least one homeowner to be present at the start and end. In Moon, most installs take one to two days depending on access, ductwork condition, and electrical upgrades—a common variable in older homes. When you're there at the start, you can confirm details about thermostat location, airflow, and any questions the crew may have about utility shut-offs or unusual spaces. At wrap-up, your installer should walk you through the equipment, thermostat operation, and answer questions before you sign off. You don’t need to supervise every minute, but your presence helps prevent miscommunication and small surprises—especially when you're spending between $5,400–$13,000 for a typical system.
How much does a furnace replacement cost in Moon? In Moon, furnace replacements usually run in the same range as an AC swap, because most companies quote furnace, coil, and AC replacement as a package. The typical installed price falls between $5,400–$13,000, depending mainly on the size, duct condition, accessibility, and whether the electrical service needs upgrading. For older, tight basements here, labor can push costs up. If just the furnace is needed, it’s sometimes possible to come in at the lower end of this band, but significant duct repairs or safety upgrades may move you higher. All quotes should specify model and include labor, permits, and a labor warranty.
Is a high-efficiency system worth the extra cost here? A high-efficiency system can be worth it in Moon—especially where fuel costs are high or if you plan to stay in your home long-term. But payback isn’t automatic, particularly in older housing where leaks and duct losses are common. Using the typical price for an upgraded heat pump ($14,000–$18,000), calculate annual energy savings using your bill and the difference in HSPF2. Unless your ducts and insulation are solid, much of the extra efficiency may be wasted. For most pre-1980 Moon homes, invest first in air sealing and duct work before paying a premium for ultra-high efficiency equipment. In newer or well-upgraded homes, the extra cost can pay for itself in heating-driven climates.
What is the R-410A refrigerant phase-down? The R-410A phase-down is a federal mandate to reduce high global warming potential (GWP) refrigerants in new HVAC systems. R-410A is still legal to service in existing systems—there’s no ban on using or repairing them in Moon or anywhere in Pennsylvania. However, new equipment now uses refrigerants like R-454B, which have a lower environmental impact. If your current system is R-410A and working fine, you don’t need to replace it because of the phase-down. Just be aware that, as R-410A becomes less common, refrigerant costs and some replacement parts could rise. Weigh those future costs if you’re facing a major repair—sometimes it can shift the math toward replacement, especially since new installs now run $8,600–$18,000.
Are duct sealing and insulation eligible for incentives? In Moon and elsewhere in Pennsylvania, utility and state incentives sometimes exist for duct sealing, insulation, and other energy-saving improvements. While state programs change often, it’s common for utilities to offer rebates for duct sealing and insulation performed as part of a qualifying HVAC project. For example, if you’re installing a new heat pump for $8,600–$18,000, it’s worth asking your contractor what rebates apply for addressing leaky ducts—a common inefficiency in Moon’s pre-1980 homes. Always confirm eligibility before starting work, as not all sealants and methods qualify, and strict documentation is required.
Why are my Moon energy bills high with a new system? High energy bills after a new HVAC install are frustratingly common in older Moon homes, even when you’ve invested in a system costing $5,400–$13,000. In most cases, the culprit isn’t the equipment, but leaky or undersized ductwork, poor insulation, or gaps around doors and windows. New systems can only deliver their rated efficiency if the duct and building envelope are good. It’s also possible your old thermostat settings don’t match your comfort or schedule needs, causing higher bills. Ask your contractor for a duct leakage test or an energy audit—they often reveal the real source of inefficiency, particularly in pre-1960 homes.
Is natural gas available throughout Allegheny County? Natural gas is widely available in Allegheny County, including in most parts of Moon, making it a common choice for heating. However, there are pockets—often more rural or new developments—where homes rely on propane or fuel oil instead. If you’re considering a new furnace or heat pump system (usually $5,400–$13,000 or more), ask your contractor to confirm gas access before designing your system. In most cases, gas line installation isn’t needed, but some edge-of-town properties may face limitations or added costs for new gas taps.
Who inspects HVAC work in Moon? In Moon, code enforcement is local. Residential HVAC work is permitted and inspected by the relevant municipal authority as required by the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code, based on the IRC. This means for most new installations, replacements, or significant changes, you need a mechanical permit and a final inspection before closing out the job—especially if you’re spending $8,600–$18,000 on a major system. Always make sure your contractor includes the permit in your quote, and that the inspection is scheduled before any final payment. Skipping inspection can lead to trouble during future home sales or insurance claims.
