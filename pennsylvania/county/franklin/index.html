HVAC costs in Franklin County — 2026

Franklin County sees HVAC needs shaped by its high rate of homeownership (38,519 owner-occupied households) and housing stock where more than half of homes (53.4%) were built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). For most, HVAC work here means replacing or upgrading older systems rather than first-time installation. Much of this housing consists of ranch and split-level homes built between 1950 and 1980, so existing ductwork often needs repair or outright replacement during a new install. In homes with aging or poorly designed ducts, a straight equipment swap may just layer new problems over old ones.

With 5,200 heating degree days and 1,250 cooling degree days, Franklin County is a heating-dominated market. Winters are reliably cold, dropping to 12 °F in design conditions, with pronounced freeze-thaw cycles. This tips the balance: choose your system for heating first, with cooling as an essential but secondary need. High-efficiency air source heat pumps are making real inroads for year-round use, but the economics and performance are most sensitive to how long winters stay cold, and how well your home holds heat.

Maintenance that actually extends system life

The most valuable HVAC maintenance you can do in Franklin County is basic but critical: change your air filter at proper intervals (usually every 1–3 months), and use the right MERV rating for your blower. Super high-MERV filters can starve an older fan for airflow and do more harm than good unless your ductwork and blower are matched for them. Coil cleaning—especially the outdoor condenser—and clearing the condensate line each spring prevent inefficient operation and damaging backups.

Refrigerant should be checked, not "topped up." In a sealed system, needing refrigerant means you have a leak. Adding refrigerant without finding and repairing that leak is not maintenance—it's deferring a more expensive failure. Ask for verification of charge, not just a bill for pounds added.

Annual service plans offered by local contractors usually cover a spring or fall inspection, filter swap, and coil check. It can be worth it if priced fair and if appointments include actual measurements, not just a visual once-over. Watch for exclusions—if the plan doesn’t cover labor or discounts on repairs, it’s often not worth the annual cost.

In Franklin County, schedule service for your AC in March or early April, and for heating systems in September—before emergency calls spike after the first heat wave or freeze.

What each system actually costs to run in Franklin County

Calculating real-world HVAC operating cost in Franklin County starts with the region’s 5,200 heating degree days and 1,250 cooling degree days. With West Penn Power serving most of the county, the rate per kilowatt-hour makes up the lion’s share of your actual heating and cooling bill. Here’s how to compare systems:

  • Efficiency rating: SEER2 (for cooling) or HSPF2 (for heat pumps) tells you how much energy is turned into cooling or heating. Higher is better, but only if the system runs long enough to use it.
  • Home’s annual load: Heating/cooling degree days translate how cold or hot our climate gets in a typical year. Multiply your home's estimated hourly heating/cooling need (from a Manual J calculation) by these degree days for an annual figure.
  • Utility rates: Your actual cost per kWh or per therm is printed on your West Penn Power bill.

For example, if your new heat pump uses 9 HSPF2 and your home needs 40 million BTUs of heat a year (common for an older, 2,000 sq ft ranch), you’d need about 4,444 kWh (40 million ÷ 9,000 = 4,444). At $0.14/kWh, that’s $622 for heating. Do the same for cooling, adjusting for SEER2 efficiency and your cooling load.

Remember, these are guidelines—envelope tightness, duct leakage, and thermostat settings all shift the outcome. A high-efficiency heat pump running on poorly sealed, uninsulated ducts will cost more to operate than a lower-efficiency unit on tight, well-insulated ductwork. Always use your own rate and load calculation for the closest estimate.

When ductless is the right answer

Ductless mini-splits excel in Franklin County’s housing stock, especially in homes built before central AC became the norm—often ranch or split-level builds from 1950–1980. These homes may have partial or poorly laid-out ducts, or none at all. If adding or replacing ducts means gutting finished rooms, a ductless system saves money, drywall, and time.

The best uses for ductless are additions, sunrooms, finished attics, basements, or rooms that never get truly comfortable on the main system. Going ductless can end years of sweating through summer in a bonus room, or shivering in a garage-turned-office.

Single-zone units, one outdoor and one indoor head, are more efficient and controllable than most multi-zone “branch box” setups. Multi-zone systems (several indoor heads on one outdoor unit) look sleek but can run into comfort and efficiency drawbacks: one oversized system trying to serve too many leaky rooms at once is often less effective than several single-zone setups placed where needed. Heads are surface-mounted and visible—some homeowners object to the aesthetic, but the alternatives are dropped ceilings or wall chases for ductwork. Condensate from each head must be drained, so smart placement is key to avoid ongoing headaches.

If your 1950s–1980s Franklin County home lacks reliable ducts, ductless isn’t just a patch—it’s often the best technical and financial solution.

R-410A phase-down: repair or replace?

The HVAC market is mid-transition away from R-410A refrigerant to low-GWP alternatives (like R-454B and R-32), following new federal rules on high-global-warming refrigerants. In Franklin County, if your system is running fine on R-410A, you can keep using it—it is not illegal to own, run, or service. Replacement is not required for existing equipment.

Where this change matters is on major repairs. R-410A remains available for service, but as supply shrinks, prices are rising. If your older system springs a refrigerant leak, topping it up could cost far more than it did a few years ago. For a major leak in a 10- to 15-year-old unit, the cost of repair is now much closer to the cost of full replacement ($4,900–$12,000 for central AC, $7,900–$17,000 for a heat pump).

If there’s no leak, just routine maintenance, you have nothing to worry about. If you face a repair and the quote for refrigerant is high, ask whether a replacement system using the new refrigerants would pay off in the long run. But no honest contractor in Franklin County will tell you a healthy R-410A unit has to be replaced just because of the refrigerant phase-down.

What size system does a Franklin County home need?

Proper sizing—based on a real load calculation, not a rule of thumb—matters more than any brand or efficiency upgrade. Oversized systems trigger rapid cycling, uneven temperatures, high humidity in summer, and shorter compressor life. In Franklin County, with 12 °F winter design temperatures, the load calculation is designed for a dependable safety margin during the coldest days, not just average weather. It needs to be done for your actual house, not just square footage.

A Manual J load calculation models heat loss and gain based on your insulation, window area, air leakage, and duct situation, customized for the local climate. Too many contractors still install whatever size they spot on the old nameplate, copying mistakes or overkill forward through decades. Even “rule of thumb” square-footage estimates that worked when energy was cheap, or ductwork was leaky, lead to wasteful oversizing today.

Always ask: “Did you run a load calculation—and can I see it?” If the answer is no, or if the contractor hedges, keep looking. In homes where previous upgrades (windows, insulation) changed the envelope, your heating and cooling needs may be lower than the old system was sized for. Getting this step right often means the difference between steady, comfortable operation and years of frustration or high bills.

What a Pennsylvania HVAC contractor must hold

Pennsylvania does not license HVAC contractors statewide. For residential jobs—including all Franklin County work—the legal requirements are set at the municipal level. Each borough or township operates its own permitting office, with the Borough of Chambersburg using its Code Enforcement Office and surrounding towns having their own systems. Residential contractors are required to maintain Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration with the state’s Office of Attorney General for jobs over $500; you can verify this by searching hic.attorneygeneral.gov.

Anyone handling refrigerant in Franklin County needs federal EPA Section 608 certification—this requirement applies regardless of local licensing rules. Mechanical permits are generally needed for new systems or major replacements in Chambersburg, and inspections are tied to those permits. For most rural townships, permit requirements are less strict but still exist for major HVAC work.

Workers’ compensation insurance is mandatory for all employers by state law. To check coverage, homeowners can look up contractors on WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). Always insist on seeing proof of active insurance, both for property and liability, before work begins. If you want to protect your rights, the required written contract and your three-day right of cancellation are enforced under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act.

Humidity and air quality in Franklin County homes

Franklin County’s winters are long and dry, while summers can be humid, bringing discomfort and potential mold if air conditioning isn’t up to the dehumidification task. Oversized AC systems or improperly set multi-speed blowers are the biggest cause of poor dehumidification—the system cools too quickly to pull moisture from the air. If you feel clammy despite the AC running, this is likely the reason.

Whole-house humidifiers help counter winter dryness, but they add ongoing maintenance: regular cleaning to prevent mineral buildup and bacteria, and inspection of water lines for leaks. Neglecting these leads to more problems than comfort. MERV filters above 8 increase filtration, but can choke older blowers not sized for the extra resistance—use only what your system is rated for.

Mechanical ventilation makes sense in tightly sealed, newer homes but is often oversold in drafty 1950–1980 Franklin County homes, where natural leakage outpaces any added ventilator. Likewise, expensive air quality add-ons such as UV lights or ionizers rarely pay off except in special health cases. For most homes, regular filter replacement and humidity control beat costly gadgets.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in Franklin County

Should I repair or replace my HVAC system in Franklin County?

In Franklin County, this comes down to system age, reliability, and repair cost. Many homes here are running units installed 15–25 years ago, especially in the 1950–1980 builds common to the area. If you’re facing a major repair—like a failed compressor or leaking coil—on a system older than 12–15 years, replacement often makes more economic sense, especially as refrigerant costs for older units rise. For example, a new central AC replacement runs $4,900–$12,000 installed, but continued repairs add up fast. Still, if your system is under 10 years old and repairs are minor, fix it and plan for eventual replacement, rather than rushing into a decision. Always ask for the cost comparison in writing, and factor in system efficiency, utility bills, and the possible need for ductwork improvements in older homes.

How does south-central Pennsylvania's climate affect system sizing?

With a heating-dominated climate—5,200 heating degree days and a winter design temperature of 12 °F—Franklin County homes need systems sized to handle real winter cold, not just mild spells. Your contractor should use Manual J calculations that account for these local extremes, instead of offshore or southern sizing shortcuts. Over-sizing to “be safe” actually leads to worse comfort and higher bills, as systems short-cycle in both summer and winter. Right-sizing is vital for both boilers and heat pumps, especially with ranch and split-level homes that may have insulation upgrades since original construction. Before signing a contract, ask to see the sizing calculation used for your home’s actual square footage, window count, and age.

Can I service my R-410A system after the refrigerant change?

Yes, you can still service your R-410A system in Franklin County despite the refrigerant transition. The move to lower-GWP refrigerants like R-454B and R-32 only affects new equipment. You are allowed to have your R-410A unit repaired and recharged as long as supplies last, and it’s not illegal to own or run your existing system. Be aware, though, that the price of R-410A is rising as suppliers shift focus. If you develop a major leak on an older system, it may then make more sense to consider replacement ($7,900–$17,000 for a ducted heat pump) versus a costly recharge. As long as your current system is running well and leak-free, there’s no need for concern about the phase-down.

Who inspects HVAC work in Franklin County?

HVAC inspections in Franklin County are handled at the municipal level. In the Borough of Chambersburg, the Code Enforcement Office checks permitted HVAC work for code compliance. Surrounding townships and boroughs also operate their own permitting offices and usually require some form of inspection for central system installation or replacement. In areas outside of these, or where municipalities have opted out, the Pennsylvania Department of Labor & Industry enforces the Uniform Construction Code. No state-wide HVAC inspection office exists for residential jobs; it’s always local. Always check with your local office before starting work, especially since unpermitted jobs may cause headaches during home sales or when filing insurance claims.

What is commissioning and why does it matter?

Commissioning means testing and verifying that your new HVAC system works as designed after installation. In Franklin County homes—especially those with aging ducts and electrical—commissioning is crucial for comfort, efficiency, and system life. A proper commissioning includes measuring system airflow, checking refrigerant charge with gauges and thermometers, and ensuring controls work. Too often, rushed or low-bid jobs skip this step, leaving homeowners with noisy, short-cycling, or uneven-performing systems. You’re paying $7,900–$17,000 for a new heat pump or $4,900–$12,000 for an AC—it is reasonable to expect commissioning documentation. Ask your contractor to provide these details as part of the job.

What does a ductless mini-split cost in Franklin County?

In Franklin County, a single-zone ductless mini-split—suitable for an addition, sunroom, or room without existing ducts—typically costs $3,200–$6,900 installed. The wide price band reflects unit capacity, whether electrical service needs upgrading, and how difficult it is to run new lines or hang wall-mounted heads in finished spaces (common in 1950–1980 era homes). Multi-zone setups, which add more indoor heads to a single outdoor unit, increase price and often lose some efficiency. For the best investment, use ductless where ducts would be expensive or disruptive to install, rather than running it head-to-head against replacement of a central system with good ducts.

Is it worth switching from oil or propane to a heat pump?

Switching from oil or propane to a ducted air source heat pump in Franklin County can be worth it, especially given the area’s heating-dominated climate and high costs for delivered fuels in rural townships. Installed prices fall in the $7,900–$17,000 range. Savings depend on your home’s insulation and whether ducts are in shape; if ducts are poorly sealed or uninsulated, much of the efficiency advantage is lost. Heat pumps deliver steady comfort and can cut heating bills compared to older oil or propane systems—especially for owners who live in the natural gas "gap" areas outside Chambersburg or Waynesboro. Consider how many cold snaps below 20 °F you see each year and whether you’ll need a backup for deep winter cold.

Why are my Franklin County energy bills high with a new system?

If you’ve recently installed a new HVAC system in Franklin County but your energy bills remain high, the root cause is often outside of the equipment: leaky or uninsulated ducts, oversized units, or an inadequate load calculation. In older ranch and split-level homes, ducts may have been compromised over decades, even hidden behind finished walls where leaks go unnoticed. A mid-efficiency system on solid ducts can outperform a high-efficiency model on leaky ones. If a full duct system needs replacement, expect a price of $8,900–$18,000. Request a duct inspection and ask if sealing and insulation could make a real difference before blaming your new unit or looking to replace it again.

Do I need a permit to replace HVAC in Franklin County?

In the Borough of Chambersburg, replacing or installing a central HVAC system typically requires a mechanical or building permit. Other Franklin County townships and boroughs have their own permitting offices and requirements—always check locally. Filter changes and minor thermostat swaps generally do not require permits in most townships. For central AC, furnace, or heat pump replacement, skipping the permit can lead to trouble selling the home, voided warranties, or insurance headaches after a claim. Contractors should pull the permit as part of the job; if yours tells you it isn’t needed, get a second opinion. It’s your protection against substandard or non-code work.

Are duct sealing and insulation eligible for incentives?

There is currently no county-wide or state HVAC-specific duct sealing and insulation incentive program listed in Franklin County’s factual data. That said, some programs tied to your electric utility or through federal offerings may help offset costs. The dollar value varies by year, utility, and program. Duct sealing can be a silent savior in the 1950–1980 vintage homes that dominate Franklin County: money spent here often brings faster comfort and real reductions in your West Penn Power bill. At a full duct system price of $8,900–$18,000, even a modest incentive can help, but always confirm current eligibility with your contractor or utility before work begins.

How much does ductwork add to an HVAC installation?

In Franklin County’s older ranch and split-level homes, adding or replacing a full duct system is often the largest portion of a total HVAC installation bill. For a typical retrofit, you’re looking at $8,900–$18,000 for just the ductwork in addition to the system price. Homes with finished basements or tight crawlspaces may approach the high end of the range, while open attics or basements help hold costs down. Duct upgrades are essential when moving from baseboard or wall heaters to a central system, or if legacy ducts are leaking or undersized for new, high-efficiency equipment. Always have your contractor assess your existing ducts before making a final choice, as money spent here can outlast multiple generations of equipment.
