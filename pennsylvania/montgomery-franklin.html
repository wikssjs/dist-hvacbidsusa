Montgomery heating and cooling prices by system type — 2026

Montgomery’s HVAC market is shaped by a high homeownership rate and a housing stock where nearly half of homes were built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). That puts most homeowners in the replacement rather than new installation market, often updating equipment in ranches and split-levels with aging ductwork. Many of these older duct systems are now due for upgrades, especially if they were originally sized for furnaces instead of modern high-efficiency systems. With 5,200 heating degree days versus just 1,250 for cooling, this is a heating-dominated climate—residents spend more on heat than on cooling. That fact pushes the system choice toward year-round heating efficiency and capacity in cold weather.

When looking at replacement costs in Montgomery, factors like existing duct condition, system tonnage, and available electrical capacity matter more than picking one brand over another. Those with solid ductwork are positioned for central replacements or heat pumps at the lower end of the price range; if that ductwork needs major repairs or replacement, costs rise quickly. Ductless mini-splits and full duct installations fill out the higher bands for homes lacking an existing distribution system.

Gas, electric or both in south-central Pennsylvania

Heating is the main load in Montgomery and the rest of Franklin County, with a winter design temperature of 12 °F and 5,200 heating degree days. In these conditions, traditional gas furnaces have a track record of delivering reliable capacity even on the coldest nights. However, cold-climate heat pumps now hold usable heat output well below freezing—far enough that the old view (“heat pumps can’t handle Pennsylvania winters”) no longer fully applies. Still, at 12 °F, a heat pump has reduced capacity and, unless the home is well-sealed, may need a backup heat source.

Natural gas is widely available in Chambersburg, Waynesboro, and Greencastle, but outlying Franklin County townships mostly use propane or oil. For those without gas, a heat pump often beats propane or oil for ongoing operating costs, especially in homes with some weatherization. In areas with natural gas service, though, gas furnaces remain attractive, especially if your ducts and returns are sized for one. Dual fuel (heat pump plus gas furnace backup) gives you efficiency most of the year but reliable output when it’s truly cold.

If you already have safe, efficient gas service on site and your main goal is lowest first cost and dependable winter comfort, the furnace is still the obvious answer. But for anyone looking to go all-electric, cut oil or propane dependence, or add central air as part of improvements, modern heat pumps are worth a close look.

Duct condition, leakage and sealing

Ductwork often gets overlooked, but it can be the biggest source of heating and cooling loss in Montgomery homes—especially those built between 1950 and 1980. Many of these houses have original duct systems sized just for a furnace, installed when energy codes were less demanding and air sealing was rarely done. Duct leakage, especially in attics or crawlspaces, wastes a chunk of your heating dollars. A duct blaster test measures how much air leaks from the system compared to its total output, identifying joints and connections where conditioned air is escaping.

If the leak is minor, professional sealing with mastic or a sealant inside the ducts can dramatically boost performance. But if you have undersized returns—a common hidden problem in split-levels—or your ducts were built for a much less efficient furnace, replacement may be the better investment. Ducts outside the living space lose heat even if they’re tight, so insulation here is critical too.

The best time to evaluate ducts is before replacing an HVAC unit. Fixing leaks or upgrading returns after the equipment’s in means more disruption and sometimes a wasted opportunity for proper sizing. In Montgomery, a well-sealed and properly sized duct system can save more on bills than jumping up a tier in equipment efficiency.

Keeping a Montgomery system running

Regular HVAC maintenance isn’t about add-ons—it’s about doing the basics right and not getting pressured into what you don’t need. In Montgomery, one of the best investments is staying on top of air filter changes. Replacing filters at the right interval (often every 1–3 months) and using a MERV rating appropriate for your blower keeps air moving and protects your equipment. Annual coil cleaning (for both evaporator and condenser), checking the condensate line to avoid clogs (which are common in humid summer stretches), and making sure your refrigerant charge is correct all go a long way.

If your system needs a refrigerant “top-off” every year, that’s a red flag for a leak—adding more without finding and fixing the problem is not real maintenance. Annual service plans in Montgomery can offer value if they include priority scheduling and don’t lock you into unnecessary parts or upsells. The best time for preventive maintenance: spring and early fall, before summer heat waves or a cold snap flood every contractor with emergency calls. A well-maintained system avoids breakdowns and will last years longer than one neglected until it fails.

Reducing the net cost of a Montgomery installation

When replacing or installing HVAC in Montgomery, there are several ways to reduce your out-of-pocket cost—but the landscape changes annually. Federal tax credits are often the largest incentive, applied when you file your taxes for the year the work was done. This is a dollar-for-dollar reduction in next year’s income tax bill, not instant savings—meaning it helps with long-term affordability, not upfront cash flow. Utility rebates, typically provided by West Penn Power or UGI Utilities (if you have natural gas), lower your invoice by sending a payment after installation and proof-of-purchase. These are more immediate but can still take a few weeks to process.

State-level programs in Pennsylvania shift year to year, and rebate amounts, qualifying equipment, and application windows all change—sometimes without much warning. For accurate, up-to-date details, check your electric or gas utility’s website for their specific energy efficiency program. The ENERGY STAR rebate finder at energystar.gov/rebate-finder is also a good tool. Never rely on outdated articles for exact rebate numbers; see those sources as your starting point and verify every incentive before you sign a contract. Always confirm eligibility up front with your contractor or by checking the official rebate pages yourself.

When an HVAC repair stops being worth it

Montgomery homeowners often ask if repairing older HVAC is worth it, or if it’s time for replacement. The key factors: system age, the cost of the current repair relative to a new system, whether you’re facing a standard part replacement or a major failure (like a heat exchanger or compressor), and if the system uses an outdated refrigerant that’s now costly. Efficiency improvements with new systems can help, but don’t always pay off quickly in a climate with just 1,250 cooling degree days per year.

If your system is under 10 years old and the issue is a blower motor or capacitor, repair is usually more sensible than replacement. But when repairs approach a third (or more) of the cost of new equipment—and the system is already over a decade old—replacement typically makes sense. Two situations leave no choice: a cracked heat exchanger, which is a safety hazard, or a compressor failure on a system beyond warranty and life expectancy. If the system works but energy bills are still high, duct sealing or envelope improvements often deliver better returns before replacing otherwise functional equipment.

Mechanical permits for HVAC work in Montgomery

Pennsylvania does not license residential HVAC contractors at the state level. Instead, contractors need Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for jobs over $500, which is issued by the state’s Office of Attorney General—always verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov before hiring. Local governments enforce permitting and inspection: in Chambersburg, the Borough Code Enforcement Office issues and reviews permits for most central HVAC work. Each township or borough in Franklin County may have its own requirements, so double-check before scheduling work. Minor jobs like filter changes or a basic thermostat swap generally do not require a permit in most Franklin County townships.

Regardless of licensing, anyone handling refrigerant must have EPA Section 608 certification—that’s a federal requirement and applies to every contractor in Pennsylvania. For insurance, all employers must carry workers’ compensation. Always request a certificate, which you can verify directly through the WCAIS system at wcais.pa.gov. Mechanical permits ensure code compliance with the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code, which follows national standards for HVAC installation and replacement. When considering a contractor, make sure they can show proof of HIC registration, insurance, and refrigerant certification, not just a business card or an ad.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in Montgomery

What does a heat pump cost installed in Montgomery?

For a typical Montgomery homeowner, installing a ducted air source heat pump falls in the $8,300–$17,500 range, depending mainly on the size of your home, the condition or presence of ductwork, and any required electrical upgrades. If you’re considering a cold-climate model—popular thanks to our winter temperatures around 12 °F—the higher end of that range applies. Prices can be higher for homes needing significant duct repair or upsizing, common in houses built between 1950 and 1980. Always seek a detailed quote tied to your exact floor plan and insulation levels, as tonnage and layout drive both cost and comfort in a heating-dominated market like Montgomery.

How much does a new HVAC system cost in Montgomery?

Replacing both the furnace and central AC in Montgomery typically costs between $5,200–$12,500 with existing usable ducts, but a full system (including ductwork for homes that need it) can reach $9,300–$18,500. Most homes in the area were built before 1980, so upgrades or modifications to older ducts are common and can drive up the final total. For homes on propane or oil, a ducted heat pump or dual fuel system could be a better long-term investment, though on natural gas the classic furnace plus AC setup is still widely chosen. Always factor in the permitting and local utility costs unique to Montgomery and Franklin County.

How cold does it get in Montgomery and does that affect heat pumps?

Montgomery’s winter design temperature is 12 °F, and we see about 5,200 heating degree days per year. This means our houses experience long, cold heating seasons. Modern cold-climate heat pumps are now designed to hold usable capacity well below freezing, but when it gets into the teens, their output does drop and backup heat may be needed—especially in drafty or older homes. For most homeowners, this makes a dual fuel setup or an efficient backup worthwhile. A properly sized cold-climate heat pump here typically comes in at $13,500–$17,500 installed. Always ask for a manual J calculation to make sure you’re getting the right unit for Montgomery’s real-world winter loads.

Do I need ENERGY STAR equipment to qualify for incentives?

In Montgomery and across Pennsylvania, many federal and utility rebates require heat pumps or air conditioners to meet ENERGY STAR or higher efficiency standards—sometimes even specific SEER2 or HSPF2 ratings. However, requirements do shift, and some utility programs may accept non-ENERGY STAR systems if they meet certain specifications. Always confirm with your contractor and your utility’s current efficiency rebate program before purchase. If you are targeting the maximum tax credit or rebate, especially for equipment priced around $8,300–$17,500 for a heat pump install, verify the exact qualifying models; don’t rely on last year’s rules.

Why are my Montgomery energy bills high with a new system?

It’s surprisingly common for homeowners in Montgomery to install new, efficient HVAC and still face high energy bills. Often, the culprit is duct leakage, restrictions, or poor sealing—especially in homes built before 1980, which are the majority locally. A leaky duct system or a drafty shell can waste a big share of your heating and cooling output. Before blaming a new system, have your contractor test the ductwork and attic insulation first. Simple fixes like sealing ducts ($300–$800) or upgrading returns can solve problems that new equipment alone won’t fix in south-central Pennsylvania’s climate.

Is a heat pump a good choice for Montgomery winters?

Modern heat pumps are a strong option for Montgomery’s climate, especially if your home is well-sealed and insulated. The shift to cold-climate models means they can now deliver efficient heat down to 12 °F and below, which matches our winter lows. If your home doesn’t have access to natural gas, a heat pump can outperform propane or oil in terms of operating cost and convenience. The install price for a cold-climate ducted heat pump runs $13,500–$17,500. For all-electric new construction, or in rural areas, heat pumps are often now the default. That said, if you have existing gas service and older, unsealed ducts, a furnace could still be the smarter pick.

Can I service my R-410A system after the refrigerant change?

Yes, in Montgomery you can still have your R-410A air conditioner or heat pump serviced. Although regulations are phasing in new lower-GWP refrigerants, there’s no ban on using, owning, or servicing R-410A equipment. Your local contractor (who must hold an EPA Section 608 certification) can legally source and add R-410A as long as it’s available, though prices may rise as supply tightens. If your system is under 10 years old and develops a refrigerant or component issue, a repair in the $300–$1,200 range is usually reasonable. Replacement only becomes mandatory if the compressor fails near end of life or if refrigerant costs become prohibitive a decade from now.

How far ahead should I schedule in Montgomery?

Spring and early fall are the best times in Montgomery to schedule HVAC installation or maintenance work, before emergency calls peak. During July heatwaves or January cold snaps, every contractor in Franklin County gets booked out for weeks. For planned system replacements or duct upgrades—which can run from $5,200–$18,500—aim to schedule at least 3–6 weeks ahead during off-peak seasons. If you’re adding new ductwork or waiting on utility rebates, build in another week or two for town permit approval. Good advance planning avoids both higher prices and stress if the weather turns.

Does Pennsylvania license HVAC contractors?

Pennsylvania does not issue a statewide HVAC license for residential contractors. Instead, all HVAC companies working in Montgomery and the rest of Franklin County must carry Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration if the job is over $500. You should always check this at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Actual HVAC work is regulated by local municipalities, and permits are typically managed by the township or borough. For work involving refrigerant (like replacing a heat pump for $8,300–$17,500), the installer also needs a federal EPA Section 608 certification. Confirm registration, insurance, and permit compliance before hiring.
