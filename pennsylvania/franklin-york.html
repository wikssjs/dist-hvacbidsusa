How much does a furnace replacement cost in Franklin, PA?

How long does an HVAC system last in Pennsylvania?
In Franklin's climate — hot, humid summers and cold winters — a well-maintained central AC system typically lasts 15-20 years. Gas furnaces last 20-25 years. Heat pumps last 15-20 years. Regular annual maintenance (tune-ups at $102-$389) significantly extends equipment life and efficiency. Signs it's time to replace: system is 15+ years old, frequent repairs, R-22 refrigerant (no longer produced), or rising energy bills despite maintenance.
When should I replace vs. repair my AC in Franklin, PA?
General rule: if your AC is over 12 years old and repair cost exceeds $1,000 (e.g., compressor failure), replacement is usually the better investment at $4304-$10760. If the system is under 10 years old and the repair is under $500-600, repair makes sense. The '5,000 rule': multiply the system's age by the repair cost — if over $5,000, replace. Also consider: R-22 refrigerant (being phased out, very expensive to recharge), rising energy bills, and uneven cooling. A licensed Franklin HVAC tech can advise after diagnosis.
What size AC system do I need for my Franklin home?
Proper AC sizing is critical — oversized systems short-cycle and cause humidity problems; undersized systems can't keep up on hot PA days. The correct method is a Manual J load calculation performed by your licensed Franklin HVAC contractor, which accounts for your home's square footage, insulation, window area, ceiling height, and local climate data. As a rough guide: 1 ton per 600-1,000 sq ft, but don't let a contractor quote based on square footage alone without a proper load calculation.

Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, reputable HVAC installation work is tied to licensing and proper handling. Contractors in PA generally must hold a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration through the Pennsylvania Attorney General’s Office—confirm it at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For gas appliances, the technician installing or connecting gas piping must hold an active PA gas piping license. For air conditioning and heat pump work that involves refrigerant handling, the installer should carry EPA Section 608 certification—ask to see the technician’s EPA 608 card before work starts.

Mechanical permits are required by most PA municipalities for HVAC replacement and new installation. A permit is typically not required for like-for-like filter changes or thermostat swaps, but replacing components that affect airflow, gas supply, electrical wiring, or refrigerant circuits usually triggers a permit. The key step: verify your specific requirements with your Franklin-area township or borough building office, because stricter inspection practices can vary locally even within York County.

For incentives, check the PA Homeowner Rebate program and utility rebates from providers such as PPL Electric, PECO, and Columbia Gas for qualifying high-efficiency heat pumps and furnaces tied to IRA Inflation Reduction Act programs. Also note that ENERGY STAR-certified equipment may qualify for federal tax credits up to 30% under Section 25C.

To verify a contractor step-by-step in Franklin: (1) confirm the PA HIC registration online; (2) request the EPA 608 card for refrigerant work; (3) obtain a certificate of insurance and proof of workers’ compensation (WC); and (4) ensure the permit application and inspection process are clearly described in your written scope.

Conversely, if your home has workable ducts, a heat pump can reduce the need for a separate summer system and deliver comfort year-round.

Bottom line for Franklin: choose based on your home’s airflow and insulation first, then decide whether you want to pay more upfront for all-in-one comfort (heat pump) or keep your proven gas heating strategy and add/replace AC.

How to choose a HVAC installation contractor in Franklin

Choosing the right contractor in Franklin isn’t just about the lowest bid—it’s about verifying credentials, confirming the scope, and making sure the system is installed to perform, not just to “fit.” First, check IICRC-related certification claims and confirm licensing status using Pennsylvania’s available online registries. Also ask for proof of current PA HIC registration (Home Improvement Contractor) and verify it through the Attorney General’s HIC lookup. For heating and cooling jobs that involve refrigerant, request EPA Section 608 certification for the technician that will handle the refrigerant lines and recovery.

Next, get 2–3 itemized written quotes. You want line items showing labor and materials (equipment model numbers, electrical components, line-set length, duct modifications, thermostat, and airflow parts). Avoid a single “lump sum” quote unless the scope is extremely clear. Carefully review exclusions: ask whether duct redesign, airflow balancing, electrical upgrades, condensate line installation, or permit fees are included. If the job touches ductwork or air handler performance, ask whether they’ll measure airflow and document the result.

Warranty matters in the real world. Look for a clear workmanship warranty length (labor) plus separate manufacturer warranties for equipment and key components. For payment scheduling, never pay more than 10–15% upfront; insist on a holdback until the job is completed and tested. Finally, make sure the contractor provides an arrival estimate and a completion window in writing, not just a “we’ll get to it soon.”

What is a SEER2 rating and why does it matter in Franklin?

SEER2 is the modern efficiency rating used for many central air conditioning systems, and it’s also used on many cooling-focused components paired with matching equipment. In Franklin and throughout Pennsylvania, SEER2 matters because you’re paying for cooling comfort in summer, and you also want equipment efficiency that matches how your home actually performs—duct leakage and return air problems can increase runtime and reduce effective comfort even if the outdoor unit has a strong SEER2 rating.

When you compare systems, look at both the SEER2 tier and the “whole system” installation. A higher SEER2 unit in a home with poor ductwork may not deliver the savings you expect. That’s why York County replacement projects often move from a straightforward equipment swap into duct corrections or airflow balancing—especially in homes built before 1980, where duct layouts and materials may not match today’s performance requirements.

For budgeting, central AC replacement with existing ducts is commonly estimated in the $4,500–$12,000 range. Choosing a higher efficiency tier can affect that range, but the bigger performance lever in older Franklin homes is usually duct condition and correct sizing.
