Ductwork: the part nobody quotes properly

In West Deer, where most homes were built before 1960, the quality and layout of existing ductwork can swing comfort—and energy bills—by a wide margin. Duct leakage is common in older homes, sometimes wasting 20% or more of conditioned air before it even reaches your living space. This isn’t just a minor efficiency loss; it can make the difference between a system that keeps you comfortable all winter and one that never quite catches up.

A duct blaster test is the best first step: it measures air leakage in your ducts by pressurizing the system and tracking how quickly air escapes. With those numbers, you can decide between sealing joints and gaps—often done with mastic and proper insulation—or replacing the ducts entirely if they’re too far gone. Don’t underestimate undersized returns, which are a sneaky and chronic problem in older homes. If your returns are too small, the blower works harder, airflow suffers, and your system ages faster.

Duct runs through unconditioned spaces like crawlspaces or uninsulated attics lose plenty of energy and can even cause condensation problems. Homes built before central air was standard may have trunk and branch ducts meant just for heating, and retrofitting for cooling or heat pumps may demand both more capacity and a new approach to air sealing—and usually electrical upgrades, too.

The most honest advice: have your ductwork tested and evaluated before investing in new equipment. Upgrading the system without knowing your duct leakage is like putting a new engine in a car with leaky tires. You might not get what you paid for.

The central decision for a West Deer homeowner

In Allegheny County, where the winter design temperature bottoms out at 8°F and we see 5,600 heating degree days each year, the single biggest decision in HVAC is how you cover your winter heating load. It’s not an academic point—our climate is squarely heating-driven. Modern cold-climate heat pumps do maintain useful output even into the teens, and the old knock that “heat pumps don’t work in cold climates” doesn’t hold up with the new generation of equipment. But as the temperatures fall, so does heat pump capacity. That’s where heat pump sizing, home insulation, and backup heat (especially gas, if you have it) become critical. Dual fuel (“hybrid”) setups use a heat pump for moderate cold and a gas furnace for the deep freeze: you get the best savings without giving up comfort during western Pennsylvania’s coldest spells.

Gas is widely available across Allegheny County, but it isn’t universal—especially as you get into more rural corners, some homes still use propane or fuel oil. Where gas is available, running a high-efficiency furnace is still often the lowest-hassle approach, with reliable operation at any temperature. If your home can’t be connected to gas, the economics shift toward heat pumps, especially all-electric systems, since propane and oil costs make heat pumps the clear favorite in total cost of ownership.

Where does a gas furnace still make sense? If you have existing gas service and an older duct system not suitable for high airflow, a furnace remains the most dependable and cost-effective route, especially for homes with challenging insulation or wiring upgrades.

R-410A phase-down: repair or replace?

As of 2026, new residential HVAC systems no longer use R-410A—major manufacturers are shifting to lower-GWP refrigerants like R-454B and R-32 under national climate policy. If your system is still running on R-410A, you aren’t required to replace it, and it’s completely legal to continue servicing and repairing these systems in West Deer and across Pennsylvania.

What’s changing is the future cost of repairs if your R-410A system ever develops a refrigerant leak. With new production of R-410A slowing, the price per pound for this refrigerant has been trending upward as supplies tighten. If your system is working, there’s no deadline or mandate to replace it. Routine maintenance and minor repairs continue as usual; no one is coming to take your system away. But if you do develop a leak, the “repair or replace” calculation shifts. At some point, the expense of a large recharge with hard-to-find R-410A may push an older system past what’s sensible to keep fixing, especially considering today’s more efficient options.

The bottom line: Existing R-410A equipment stays legal and serviceable in Allegheny County. It’s the new equipment market and long-run economics that are changing—not what you’re allowed to own or repair.

What a Pennsylvania HVAC contractor must hold

Pennsylvania does not issue a statewide HVAC license for residential contractors. Instead, home HVAC work is regulated locally—municipalities enforce mechanical permits, code compliance, and who can pull permits in their jurisdiction. There’s no central board or license covering all residential HVAC, and the requirements can shift depending on whether you’re in a city, township, or borough. Commercial and residential regulations split at the local level; most homeowners have never run into commercial code requirements, so double-check your contractor knows residential code in your area.

What is enforced for everyone is Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration, required for contracts over $500 and tracked by the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. For any job that touches refrigerant lines, EPA Section 608 certification is required—federal, not state, so it applies everywhere. Mechanical permits are local, requested from your municipality, and should cover inspections both before and after work. Proof of workers’ compensation coverage is mandatory for all employers, and you can verify active policy status through the state’s WCAIS database. Insurance coverage should be provided in writing and updated on request.

In Allegheny County, ask your installer for permit receipts, documentation of HIC registration, and technician-specific refrigerant certification at minimum.

Zone control without ductwork

Ductless mini-split systems are a perfect fit for West Deer’s older homes, where much of the existing housing stock predates central air—and where running new ductwork means tearing into vintage plaster or navigating cramped attic eaves. For additions, attic or basement conversions, enclosed porches, or rooms over garages, ductless solves the cost and disruption of full duct installation.

Single-zone systems are the lowest-cost option at $3,200–$7,100 installed, and often deliver better efficiency than multi-zone setups. Each indoor head operates independently, allowing you to set different temperatures room by room and skip heating or cooling spaces you’re not using—all without the thermal losses common to ductwork. Multi-zone systems do have one clear advantage: one outdoor unit can serve several rooms, but they often lose some efficiency and heating power per head compared to multiple single-zone units. Be sure to consult on whether your comfort goals are best met by several separate systems or a single outdoor unit with multiple zones.

Many homeowners hesitate over the look of mini-split heads on their walls, but experienced installers can usually find locations that minimize visual impact and still deliver good airflow. Condensate drainage should be planned before installation—gravity drains are neatest, but pumps are used where needed.

Bottom line: For homes built before the 1960s—where retrofitting ducts is impractical—ductless is often the only sensible way to get zoned comfort without wrecking your home’s original finishes.
