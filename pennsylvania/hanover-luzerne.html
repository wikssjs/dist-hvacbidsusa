In Hanover, the HVAC market is shaped by older homes and a high homeownership rate. With 67.8% of households owning their homes and 76.8% of housing stock built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), much of the local work is retrofit or replacement—not new installation. Many Luzerne County homes originally had coal or oil boilers and radiators, so today's HVAC projects frequently involve converting older heating systems to forced-air and, if central cooling is added, installing or upgrading ducts as well. Replacement costs vary widely depending on whether you have existing, usable ductwork or need a full retrofit.

Northeastern Pennsylvania is a heating-dominated climate, with 6,100 heating degree days and just 850 cooling degree days per year. That makes heating efficiency the major driver of your yearly costs, while central AC is often more about comfort during late-summer stretches. If your house still runs on a decades-old furnace or boiler, a switch to a modern heat pump or high-efficiency furnace can lower bills, but older homes may need ductwork upgrades or electrical panel work to handle new loads. Manual J and the tonnage question

The number one mistake I see in Hanover is oversized HVAC equipment, usually because an installer took a shortcut on sizing—or copied the old unit’s sticker. Oversizing is not a favor. It leads to short cycling, poor humidity control, uneven temperatures and, in the long run, a system that wears out faster and costs more. The “bigger is better” approach doesn’t work for HVAC. Heating and cooling systems should be just large enough to meet the home’s needs on the coldest (or hottest) typical design day. In Hanover, that design day is 4 °F for winter—any reputable contractor should base sizing on this, not just square footage or a hunch.

The gold standard is the Manual J load calculation, which accounts for your home’s actual insulation, window type and area, air leakage, orientation, and more. Two houses on the same block can have totally different heat loss if one has storm windows and modern insulation and the other doesn’t. Old rules of thumb (“one ton per 500 sq ft,” etc.) can miss these differences—and so can a company that only references the nameplate of your old furnace or AC. That just perpetuates errors from decades past. When you talk to a contractor, always ask: “Did you run a Manual J load calculation, and can I see it?” It’s the best way to ensure you’re not paying now for a mistake that will cost you in years of discomfort and excess repairs.

The refrigerant change and what it means for repairs

In the last several years, the refrigerant landscape has shifted. Newly manufactured residential HVAC systems now use refrigerants with lower global warming potential (GWP) like R-454B and R-32 as part of a phasedown of older high-GWP HFCs. However, R-410A systems already installed in Hanover homes are not illegal and remain serviceable. There’s no need to replace your AC or heat pump just because of this change.

What does this mean if you have an existing R-410A system? Repairs—especially recharges—are still possible, but over time, the cost of R-410A refrigerant will likely rise as supplies tighten. The key impact is economic: if your system develops a significant leak or needs extensive repairs that involve recharging with R-410A, the price of refrigerant may push the repair cost closer to the threshold where replacement makes sense. This doesn’t mean you should preemptively swap out a working unit, but it does mean future repairs might change your cost-benefit decision. The change mostly impacts new HVAC installations and long-term repair economics—not the legality of your old system.

Hanover's housing stock and its HVAC needs

Hanover’s housing is shaped by the pre-1980 and especially 1900–1940 boom: many homes were built for coal heat and later retrofitted for oil or gas. Forced-air ductwork, if present, was often added decades after original construction and may be sized for heat only—not for central cooling, which needs larger ducts to handle airflow without excess noise or room-to-room imbalances. If your home has old radiators and no ducts, adding central AC or a heat pump means a full ductwork install, which is a significant expense and disruption.

Insulation and window efficiency in this housing stock range widely. Many homes still have single-pane or early double-pane windows, and basic attic insulation—improving these, when feasible, can sometimes be a better investment than a hyper-efficient HVAC unit. Chimneys and flues in older homes can complicate gas furnace or boiler replacement, especially if old masonry is crumbling. Electrical capacity is another challenge for all-electric conversions like heat pumps: homes wired before the 1970s may have limited panel space or undersized service. These realities drive both price and system selection. Few projects are “drop-in” jobs here.

Permits, licensing and inspections in Hanover

In Hanover and the rest of Luzerne County, state-level HVAC licensing does not exist for residential work. Instead, Pennsylvania requires all contractors performing contracts over $500 to carry Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration, which is issued by the Office of Attorney General—not a trade exam. Municipalities handle permit and inspection for residential HVAC projects; in Wilkes-Barre, you’ll nearly always need a mechanical permit and city inspection for a new install or major replacement, but plug-in space heaters are used without permits.

Federal EPA Section 608 certification is required for anyone working with refrigerant, including heat pumps and ACs. This is a technician credential, not company-wide. Always ask your contractor for their HIC number and verify it on the official site. For homeowners wanting to check insurance and workers' comp, the WCAIS portal will show if a contractor is properly covered—do not skip this, as Pennsylvania mandates workers’ compensation for all employers. Written contracts are required by state law for any project over $500, and you get three business days to cancel. If your home was built before 1978, lead-safe certification applies for any remodel disturbing painted surfaces, per the federal EPA rule.

Efficiency ratings and what they mean for your bill

HVAC efficiency ratings can look like a jumble of numbers: SEER2 for cooling, HSPF2 for heat pumps, and AFUE for furnaces. SEER2 (Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio 2) tells you how efficiently a system cools, while HSPF2 (Heating Seasonal Performance Factor 2) ranks heat pump heating. Both standards replaced their earlier forms in recent years, and old figures found online aren’t apples-to-apples with what’s sold today.

For Hanover and Luzerne County, heating is the big expense. With 6,100 heating degree days but just 850 cooling degree days, paying extra for a top-rated SEER2 is unlikely to pay off—16–18 SEER2 is more than enough. On the heat pump side, though, HSPF2 does matter: stepping up from the minimum to a unit in the 9–10 range will lower winter bills noticeably and pay for itself over several seasons. The very highest HSPF2 models have a diminishing return, but a mid-to-upper tier often makes sense. In short: focus on heating efficiency if you want a real impact on annual utility costs, unless your home is unusually hard to cool.

Filtration, humidification and ventilation

Hanover’s cold winters bring dry indoor air—from mid-winter on, a forced-air furnace can leave your house at 20% humidity or less, leading to dry skin, static and discomfort. Summer brings the opposite challenge: high humidity that lingers without proper AC or dehumidification. Oversized air conditioners, common in older homes, cool the air too quickly but don’t run long enough to pull moisture out—so mold and clamminess can persist even with the thermostat set low.

Whole-house humidifiers can give winter relief but require attentive cleaning to avoid mineral buildup and mold inside ducts. High-efficiency filters (MERV 13 and above) can trap allergens but strain older blowers not sized for added resistance—ask if your furnace can handle it before upgrading. In newer or tightly renovated homes, mechanical ventilation—like an HRV or ERV—can help with stale air and moisture, but in Hanover’s older, draft-prone homes, infiltration often provides enough fresh air already. Many indoor air quality “add-ons” are oversold locally. For most Hanover homes, a decent filter, a regular maintenance schedule, and spot humidification or dehumidification cover most real needs.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in Hanover

Do I need ENERGY STAR equipment to qualify for incentives? ENERGY STAR is a widely recognized efficiency mark, and in Hanover, some utility rebates and federal programs are tied to purchasing certified equipment. However, not every incentive in Pennsylvania requires the ENERGY STAR label—they may instead set minimum efficiency thresholds, such as SEER2 or HSPF2 ratings. Before making a purchase, check the actual incentive terms offered by your electric or gas utility (for most here, that's PPL Electric Utilities or UGI Utilities). Sometimes a high-efficiency system qualifies for a rebate even if it’s not marked ENERGY STAR, if it meets the required performance. For example, a ducted heat pump costing $7,600–$16,000 can be eligible for significant rebates if its HSPF2 or SEER2 meets the incentive level. Be sure to confirm those numbers with your contractor.
What is EPA Section 608 certification? In Hanover and throughout Pennsylvania, EPA Section 608 certification is a federal requirement for anyone handling refrigerants—such as during the installation, service, or disposal of air conditioners and heat pumps. It makes sure technicians have been properly trained to handle, recover and dispose of refrigerants in a way that protects health and the environment. Every tech who puts gauges on your cooling system is legally required to have Section 608; it’s not just paperwork, and it doesn’t substitute for state or municipal registration. When you hire a contractor for any job involving refrigerant—whether it’s a new central AC system or a ductless mini-split install costing $3,100–$6,700—ask to see proof that the installer or service technician has current Section 608 credentials.
How much does a furnace replacement cost in Hanover? For furnace-only replacement, prices are highly dependent on the fuel type (natural gas, propane or oil), but if you look at the price bands for comparable forced-air systems, a typical central furnace swap in a Hanover home with existing ductwork lines up closely to the AC bands—generally running in the $4,800–$11,500 range installed. Houses with complicated venting, dirty or leaky ducts, or legacy conversions from radiators may see costs at the higher end. If your home is one of many older houses originally built with steam or hot water heat, ductwork condition and the need for chimney lining can add to the price. Always ensure you have a detailed written quote covering these realities before you sign.
How much does ductwork add to an HVAC installation? In Hanover’s older housing stock, where ducts were often added long after original construction (or don’t exist at all), installing new ductwork is a substantial added expense. For a full, professionally installed duct system sized for both heating and cooling, expect to pay $8,600–$17,000. Much of the cost comes from routing runs through thick plaster walls, floors, or attics—especially if your home was never designed for forced air. For smaller jobs or partial system replacements, costs may be lower, but there’s no shortcut around labor in houses built before modern HVAC standards.
What does HSPF2 measure? HSPF2, or Heating Seasonal Performance Factor 2, is the efficiency rating used for modern ducted and ductless heat pumps. It measures how much heating a system provides over an entire season compared to how much electricity it uses. In a climate like Hanover’s—with 6,100 heating degree days—HSPF2 is crucial because most of your annual HVAC energy goes to staying warm. New systems typically score 8–11 HSPF2. Boosting your system’s HSPF2 by a point or two can save you real money over a winter. For example, if you’re choosing between two ducted heat pumps costing $7,600–$16,000, the one with a higher HSPF2 rating will likely cost less to run after installation, especially over time.
Which utility serves Hanover? In Hanover and throughout most of Luzerne County, electric service is provided by PPL Electric Utilities, and if your home has natural gas, the provider is generally UGI Utilities. This matters because PPL’s electric rates affect what you pay to run AC or heat pumps, and UGI’s rates are key for natural gas furnace or boiler heating. Utility incentives are sometimes available for high-efficiency HVAC systems—such as ductless mini-splits costing $3,100–$6,700—so it’s worth checking with your utility about current offers before signing a contract or committing to a system type.
Why does my system short cycle? In Hanover, short cycling is usually the result of an oversized system—a problem often inherited from contractors skipping a proper load calculation, especially in older homes with inconsistent insulation. When an HVAC unit is too large, it heats or cools your rooms too quickly, shuts off before it’s run long enough to balance temperatures or remove humidity, and then starts up again moments later. Over time, this leads to higher bills, more wear-and-tear, and less comfort. Whether you have a new air source heat pump ($7,600–$16,000) or an old furnace, make sure your system is sized for your home’s actual heat loss—not just square footage or an old unit’s specs—to avoid this common and fixable issue.
Should I replace the furnace and AC at the same time? In Hanover’s older housing stock, replacing both the furnace and AC together can make sense—especially if both are at the end of their expected life, share ductwork, or if you’re considering a switch to a heat pump that covers both heating and cooling. Many local homes have ductwork and blower assemblies designed for units from a different era; mismatched components can result in poor performance, extra noise, or even system failure. If you’re spending in the $4,800–$11,500 range for each side of the system, a packaged replacement can sometimes offer cost savings and peace of mind. However, if one system is still in good condition, replacing just that component is fine—don’t let a salesperson talk you into premature upgrades.
How long does HVAC installation take in Hanover? For most straightforward replacements of central AC or furnace units in Hanover homes where solid ductwork exists, a typical installation takes one to two full workdays. Complex projects—such as switching from radiator (boiler) heat to new forced air, or a full install with ductwork ($8,600–$17,000)—can stretch to several days or even a week, particularly in older houses with tough access or minimal existing infrastructure. Weather, building inspections, and the unique layout of your home can all add time. Expect some disruption, but reputable contractors in Luzerne County work efficiently and will spell out a project timeline in their contract before starting.
