Retrofit versus replacement in Lansdowne

If you live in Lansdowne, the odds are high your house was built before 1980—and most likely as a rowhouse or twin, not a new suburban detached home. HVAC options here are shaped entirely by that. First: older homes rarely have supply and return ducts sized for modern air conditioning. Many were built with gravity furnaces or very basic trunk and branch ductwork, enough for heating but poorly sealed and not insulated for cooling loads. Adding central air to these homes without new ducts often leads to uneven cooling, hot rooms upstairs, and high bills.

Insulation and window glazing varies from minimal to upgraded, but most pre-1980 homes need envelope work before going all-electric. Electric panels may be 100 amps or lower, so a full heat pump conversion sometimes demands a service upgrade—a meaningful cost and disruption. If you’re considering replacing a gas furnace in these older homes, check the chimney or flue: oversized or unlined chimneys are common and may need a liner. Newer areas in Delaware County see more straightforward replacements, but most Lansdowne work is about adapting to what’s there, not replacing builder-grade units from the 1990s.

Bottom line: your existing infrastructure—ducts, panel, and insulation—drives both price and what’s possible here. A good contractor will assess all of these before quoting new HVAC.

When ductless is the right answer

Ductless mini-splits solve a lot of headaches in Lansdowne’s older homes. In 1920–1950 rowhouses and twins, you’ll often find no existing space for full ductwork—and installing it would mean cutting into plaster, lowering ceilings, or losing closet space. Ductless units are the shortest path to comfort in finished attics, additions, and basements where running new ducts makes no sense. They shine in rooms that never got proper airflow or where you're tired of window units every summer.

Single-zone systems (one outdoor, one indoor unit) are usually most efficient and reliable. Multi-zone systems run several heads off one outdoor unit and offer room-by-room control, but with some efficiency loss and less fine-tuned comfort when loads differ between rooms. Visual concerns—wall-mounted heads—are real, but worth weighing against construction costs and disruption. Condensate (“drain”) lines must run somewhere safe; this takes planning in finished spaces.

In Lansdowne, the reality is that a large portion of older housing stock was never built for central air. That makes ductless not a luxury, but often the only practical tool for the job—especially for cooling. If a contractor insists on cutting the house apart to install a traditional ducted system, get a second opinion.

Mechanical permits for HVAC work in Lansdowne

Pennsylvania does not issue a state HVAC license. For residential work in Delaware County—including Lansdowne—HVAC projects are regulated at the municipal level. That means the borough or township building department governs permits, plan review, and inspections. Some local governments require mechanical permits for any new or replacement HVAC system, especially for central systems, while smaller work (like a thermostat swap in Haverford Township) often happens without a permit.

Every legitimate contractor working in your home must have a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration from the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General if the contract is over $500. You can verify this at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Any worker handling refrigerants—installing, servicing, or charging air conditioners and heat pumps—must also hold a federal EPA Section 608 certification, no exceptions. For insurance, all employers are required to carry workers’ compensation in Pennsylvania; you can check their insurance status through the state’s WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov).

If your job requires a permit, work should not be started until it’s pulled and posted. Final inspections protect you and are mandatory in most municipalities for new HVAC systems.

Separating installers from equipment sellers

In Lansdowne’s crowded HVAC market, you’ll find contractors of every description—some focused on installation quality, others just moving boxes. It’s easy to buy an expensive system and end up with poor comfort if the install is rushed or corners are cut. Installation quality matters more than brand; the best equipment works badly if airflow is off, refrigerant charge is wrong, or ducts leak.

Start by verifying a contractor’s HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Ask if your job will be handled by EPA Section 608–certified techs if it involves refrigerants. Always demand a detailed quote: it should include a load calculation (not a “rule of thumb” guess), specific model numbers, scope for any ductwork or electrical upgrades, and a commissioning process—meaning the installer measures and documents airflow, static pressure, and refrigerant charge after startup.

Red flags: In a market like Delaware County, with dense contractor presence and little rural travel cost, high-pressure sales tactics or dramatic price differences usually signal poor installation or missing permits, not real savings.

The central decision for a Lansdowne homeowner

Lansdowne winters aren’t Arctic, but with a winter design temperature of 14 °F and 4,800 heating degree days each year, heating is the main event. Older objections to heat pumps (“they don’t keep up in cold climates”) are less relevant—modern cold-climate models keep useful capacity well below freezing. But the math here is different than in the South: as it gets colder, even the best heat pump loses efficiency and capacity. Dual-fuel systems—heat pumps backed up by gas—are common for this exact reason.

Most Lansdowne homes have access to natural gas, with PECO as the utility in most inner-ring Delaware County townships. Gas is still the go-to for lowest-cost heat, especially if your home isn’t tightly sealed or your electrical panel is undersized for a larger heat pump install. Further out where only propane or oil are options, heat pumps look more attractive—they beat delivered fuels in both efficiency and safety.

A furnace is still the best call where gas is cheap and already set up, especially in leaky old homes or where you want redundancy for deep cold snaps. But if you’re looking at a major duct or equipment overhaul—or aiming for a future-proof, all-electric setup—a ducted or ductless heat pump, or a dual-fuel setup, may make more sense.

The decision point on an aging system

Deciding between repair and replacement in Lansdowne means weighing system age, repair cost, and the root cause of failure. Don’t replace good equipment for minor issues, and don’t pour money into a system at the end of its useful life. Most central HVAC systems last 12–18 years in our climate—less if neglected, longer with proper care.

If your system is under 10 years old and the failure is a board, capacitor, or single part—not a compressor or heat exchanger—repair usually makes the most sense, especially if it’s under $1,200. When repair costs climb past a third of the full replacement price and your system is 10–15 years old, start pricing new systems instead. If your system uses old R-22 refrigerant or has mounting reliability issues, that tips the scales toward replacement.

Replacement is not optional in two cases: a cracked heat exchanger (it’s a carbon monoxide risk) and compressor failure past warranty on an older unit. Upgrading strictly for efficiency only pays off if your ductwork and insulation are already sorted—in this climate, you’ll rarely save enough on bills to justify replacing a working system solely for higher SEER or HSPF.

Should I close vents in unused rooms? In most Lansdowne homes—especially older ones—closing supply vents in unused rooms is not a good idea. Your ductwork was (usually) not designed for variable airflow. Closing off rooms increases pressure in the ducts, which can boost leakage, stress the blower, and actually decrease overall comfort. It may even reduce your system’s effective efficiency, especially if ducts run through unconditioned attics or basements. Instead, consider zoning upgrades or ductless mini-splits for targeted comfort; a single-zone ductless unit for a problem room runs about $3,100–$6,800 installed in Delaware County. If certain rooms are always uncomfortable, have your ducts checked for air leaks and improper sizing before shutting vents.

What MERV filter rating should I use? In Lansdowne’s climate, a MERV 8 filter is suitable for most homes, balancing air quality with airflow in older systems.
