Manual J and the tonnage question

The most common (and costly) HVAC mistake in Conshohocken is oversizing—installing a system that’s too big for the house. Many believe “bigger is better,” but an oversized system creates short cycling (rapid on/off cycling), inconsistent temperatures, poor humidity control and a shorter equipment lifespan. It’s not a safety margin; it’s a defect that wastes money and comfort.

The right way to size equipment is a Manual J load calculation. This procedure looks at your specific house—window type and orientation, insulation, number of occupants, duct leakage, even Sun exposure. Rules of thumb based on square footage, or simply copying the old system’s sizing, ignore decades of improvements or—worse—repeat sizing errors from past installers. If a contractor doesn’t run a fresh calculation using today’s details, you could be setting up for poor performance for another 15 years.

Montgomery County’s winter design temp of 14°F is the benchmark for heating capacity. A Manual J calculation uses this the way a stress test uses your maximum heart rate—it ensures your new equipment can handle the worst days, but not drain your wallet and comfort the rest of the year.

When getting a quote, ask plainly: “Did you run a Manual J load calculation, and can I see it?” It’s the single most revealing question to separate a true pro from a shortcut taker.

Filtration, humidification and ventilation

Conshohocken’s climate is two-faced: our winters are dry enough to cause cracked skin and static, but summer brings heavy, sticky humidity. Modern, tightly sealed homes built or remodeled since the 1980s require smarter approaches to filtration and ventilation than older, draftier houses.

Overactive air conditioning—often the result of oversized AC or heat pumps—can cool the house quickly but leave unchecked humidity. Ideal dehumidification comes from equipment that runs long enough at the right speed; bigger isn’t better. Whole-house humidifiers do solve winter dryness but demand annual maintenance and careful operation to avoid mold and minerals. If you’re busy or forgetful, a good portable humidifier in the bedrooms may serve just as well for less hassle.

Filtration gets oversold too. Media filters rated MERV 8–11 usually strike the right balance between air quality and blower stress for most homes. Pushing higher MERV ratings can choke airflow in many older Conshohocken systems. If you’re considering higher ratings, make sure your blower motor can handle it.

Mechanical ventilation—HRVs or ERVs—is worth considering if your home is tightly sealed and you see condensation, stuffiness, or persistent odors. For most older homes here, passive ventilation (windows, bath fans) is usually enough unless you’ve completed major weatherization work. Add-ons like UV lights and electronic air cleaners see lots of sales pitches, less proven benefit per dollar.

Keeping a Conshohocken system running

Most of what extends HVAC lifespan in Conshohocken isn’t a long list. Filter changes—on schedule, using the right MERV rating for your blower—are worth more than most gadgets. Clean indoor and outdoor coils help keep efficiency up. Periodically flush the condensate drain, especially during our humid summers, to prevent backups.

A proper maintenance visit should verify your system’s refrigerant charge. But “topping up” alone—adding refrigerant every year—is not maintenance, it masks a leak. If refrigerant is ever low, a real technician looks for and fixes the leak, period.

Annual HVAC service plans are everywhere. Some are worth it—if they include legitimate inspection and cleaning, priority scheduling, and a documented maintenance checklist. Watch out for plans that amount to a rushed visit and a coupon for work you didn’t need. If you’re reasonably diligent about filters and care, paying out of pocket once every year or two often yields the same results for less.

Best time for routine maintenance is spring or fall, before the true heating and cooling rush. You’ll get better availability and more attention than in a summer or winter emergency slot.

Mechanical permits for HVAC work in Conshohocken

Pennsylvania has no statewide license for HVAC contractors; instead, registration as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) is required for contracts exceeding $500, regardless of trade. Individual towns, including those in Montgomery County, govern mechanical permits for residential work—so the authority is local, not state, for your home in Conshohocken. For replacements or installations requiring new electrical or mechanical connections, a permit is typically required.

Any technician handling refrigerant must hold EPA Section 608 certification, which is federal and not optional for any work involving AC or heat pumps around Conshohocken. Always verify that your contractor’s HIC registration is active at hic.attorneygeneral.gov before hiring.

Written contracts are mandatory over $500 and you have a three-day right to cancel under Pennsylvania law. Your contractor—if employing workers—must carry workers’ compensation insurance, which can be checked through WCAIS at wcais.pa.gov. Never accept a bid from anyone unwilling to provide proof of HIC registration and insurance, or who suggests waiving your home insurance deductible. These are non-negotiables for any legitimate Montgomery County job.

Duct condition, leakage and sealing

For many homes in Conshohocken—and particularly those post‑1945 suburban homes common in Montgomery County—the state of your ducts can matter more than the efficiency of your furnace or AC. Older ductwork, often retrofitted into homes built before central AC was standard, may be undersized, poorly sealed or even missing entirely in some rooms. This means leakage rates can waste a significant part of your heated or cooled air, especially when duct runs go through unconditioned attics or crawlspaces.

Testing duct performance isn’t guesswork. A duct blaster test quantifies exactly how much air escapes before reaching your living spaces. Results can drive real decision-making—if leakage is moderate, targeted sealing (with mastic, not duct tape) may be all you need. Where returns are undersized or some rooms are always “off” in temperature, a full redesign or even replacement could be the answer—but there’s no reason to jump to that before testing.

In the post-war housing found all over Abington, Cheltenham, and Upper Dublin, original ducts were often sized just for heating, with return air paths improvised or inadequate compared to modern airflow standards. These systems are rarely sealed to today’s expectations. Before committing to new equipment—and especially before switching from a furnace to a heat pump—have your ducts tested and evaluated. Fix the hidden distribution problems first and you’ll get every bit of performance you paid for the next time around.

What size HVAC system does my Conshohocken home need? There’s no single answer for Conshohocken homes, and anyone who quotes tonnage based solely on square footage or your old unit’s size is guessing. The only sound way is with a Manual J load calculation, which factors in insulation, windows, orientation, air leakage and even duct conditions (especially for the 61.1% of homes built before 1980). For reference, a typical postwar Montgomery County home might need a 2.5 to 3-ton system, but small changes—duct leakage, new insulation, or that big picture window—have big impacts. Always ask for the calculation, not just a number. Sizing correctly ensures your system lasts longer and works efficiently, whether your project’s in the $5,600–$13,500 or $8,900–$19,000 range.
Is it worth switching from oil or propane to a heat pump? Switching from oil or propane to a heat pump in Conshohocken can make financial sense—particularly with natural gas widely available, but there are circumstances where a heat pump really shines. Compared to oil or propane’s price swings, heat pumps provide stable, often lower operating costs and no onsite fuel deliveries. Full conversion costs with ducted systems usually land in the $8,900–$19,000 range. If your ductwork is sound and your house isn’t a leaky draft box, a modern cold-climate heat pump can heat all winter even in our 14°F lows. How do I verify an HVAC contractor's insurance?
