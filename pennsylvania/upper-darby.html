Zone control without ductwork

Many Upper Darby and Haverford homes—especially the 1920–1950 rows and twins—were built decades before central air was standard. That leaves most with either old radiators, gravity furnaces, or electric baseboard, and no hidden chaseways for full ductwork. In these homes, ductless mini-splits are not just an alternative; they’re often the easiest and least invasive solution for new cooling or supplemental heating.

Single-zone mini-splits are ideal when you have one area—a converted attic, finished basement, or addition—that needs separate control or isn’t reached by the main system. The unit cools or heats that zone only, maximizing efficiency and eliminating the cost of running new ducts (which in a plaster-walled rowhouse can be prohibitive).

Multi-zone systems connect several indoor heads to a single outdoor compressor. This appears tidier than stacking multiple outdoor units, but each indoor head shares capacity, so you may end up with lower output per room and less balanced performance when many zones call for cooling at once. It’s also harder to optimize temperature room by room, and repair for one zone can take down the whole multi-zone system.

Common objections are the look of the indoor head (usually wall-mounted), and handling condensate (every head needs reliable drainage). Hiding linesets can add cost, but in homes where ducts would wreck old plaster or woodwork, ductless preserves what makes the house unique.

In the Upper Darby market, if you’re not thrilled about ducts, you’re not alone—more than half the houses here were not built for them, and ductless mini-splits are a proven retrofit.

Heat pump or furnace: what makes sense in Upper Darby

With roughly 4,800 heating degree days and a winter design temperature of 14 °F, Upper Darby sits firmly in a heating-driven market. A modern cold-climate heat pump can now deliver meaningful heat well below freezing, so the old rule—that heat pumps "don’t work in Pennsylvania winters"—is obsolete unless you’re referring to systems from decades past.

Still, heat pump output drops as outside temperature drops. At 14 °F, you want either a very well-sized cold-climate model, or a backup system for true cold spells. That’s why some homeowners in our area go “dual fuel”—a heat pump covers most heating and all cooling, and a gas furnace automatically takes over when it’s below the heat pump’s cutoff.

Natural gas from PECO is widely available in Delaware County’s urbanized neighborhoods, making a gas furnace the easy replacement in homes already piped, especially if the home’s ducts and electrical panel are in good shape. In the more rural or outer areas where gas isn’t an option, you’re comparing a heat pump to oil or propane—which almost always makes the heat pump more appealing from both efficiency and maintenance perspectives.

Furnaces still make sense as a straight replacement where ducts exist, the home is already on gas, and someone wants the highest possible heat output for the lowest upfront cost. But if you’re updating or expanding, especially in homes built before central air was common, a heat pump or ductless is worth serious thought.

Mechanical permits for HVAC work in Upper Darby

In Pennsylvania, there is no statewide HVAC contractor licensing for residential work. Instead, HVAC work in homes falls under the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), based on the International Residential Code (IRC), but enforcement happens locally. In Upper Darby Township, the Department of Licenses and Inspections handles permitting, so you need a mechanical permit for any new or replacement central HVAC system installation.

Contractors must carry a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration issued by the state Attorney General for any contract over $500. This isn’t a technical license, but it’s required for consumer protection and can be checked at the state’s online registry. For refrigerant work—installing, charging, or servicing any air conditioner or heat pump—the installer must hold EPA Section 608 certification, which is a federal rule.

All employers must have workers’ compensation insurance in Pennsylvania. You can verify a contractor’s active policy through WCAIS, the state’s insurance database. If you live in a pre-1978 house, federal rules around lead paint may also apply for certain projects—always check before major disturbance.

For anything more than simple thermostat swaps or filter changes, expect a reputable contractor to pull the right permit and provide you copies of registration and insurance. If they try to skip those steps, they aren’t playing by Upper Darby’s rules.

Refrigerant rules and your existing system

Recent federal rules are accelerating the switch away from high-global warming potential (GWP) refrigerants. As of 2024, new residential HVAC equipment uses lower-GWP refrigerants like R-454B or R-32. These are safer for the environment and meet new product standards, but your existing R-410A system remains legal, is not grandfathered “out of code,” and can still be recharged or maintained.

For homeowners in Upper Darby with a cooling system installed in the past decade, you’re almost certainly running on R-410A. The gap comes when a repair requires significant refrigerant: as the supply of R-410A tightens, the cost of a recharge or top-off rises, especially if you have a slow leak that was never properly fixed. At some point, the added service cost starts to make replacing an old, leaky system with a new system (in the $4,800–$11,500 range for central AC) the smarter call.

It’s worth underscoring: You do not have to rip out a working system just because refrigerant rules changed. Your existing equipment stays legal and serviceable. It just means you compare the economics more carefully as either the age of your unit or repair bills rise.

What each system actually costs to run in Upper Darby

To compare what it costs to run a gas furnace, a heat pump, or a ductless system in Upper Darby, use the actual climate: 4,800 heating degree days (heating is the main event) and 1,450 cooling degree days each year. PECO Energy Company is your likely electric (and for most, natural gas) utility here, and your bill has a “per kWh” or “per therm” usage rate you can use for the math below.

Step one: Find your rate on your last PECO bill. Let’s say electricity is $0.18 per kWh (you’ll use your actual rate), and natural gas is $1.40 per therm. Next, check system efficiency: heat pumps are rated HSPF2 (heating) and SEER2 (cooling); furnaces are AFUE.

For heating: A ducted heat pump rated 9.5 HSPF2 delivers 9,500 BTUs per kWh used; a modern gas furnace at 95% AFUE yields 95,000 BTUs of heat per 100,000 BTUs of gas burned. Use the heating degree days, square footage, and duct leakage: a tight, insulated 1,600 sq ft rowhouse with 4,800 HDD needs less heat than a bigger, older home with leaky ducts.

Let’s say you use 50 million BTUs for heating per season. Divide that by your system’s efficiency to get your energy purchased, then multiply by your rate. If ducts leak or insulation is lacking, your real costs go up fast—no rating can “save” a bad delivery setup.

Bottom line: If you want savings, invest first in duct repair and air sealing. High efficiency in a poorly sealed or leaky system always underperforms the marketing numbers. Your utility bill is the only number that counts.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in Upper Darby

Should I close vents in unused rooms?

In Upper Darby’s older rowhomes and twins, it’s tempting to close supply vents in rooms you don’t use to "save energy." However, this isn’t recommended. Your central HVAC system was designed for a specific airflow and pressure balance. Shutting vents can increase duct leakage—especially in aging ductwork—or create backpressure that strains the blower motor, leading to higher energy use or even system damage. In some cases, closed vents can actually increase air leakage into unconditioned spaces, which means the system works harder. If you want more precise zone control, consider a ductless mini-split, especially for spaces that never warmed or cooled properly. For these, installing a dedicated unit is usually in the $3,100–$6,800 range per zone. The best move is to leave all vents open and focus on sealing and insulating to increase efficiency.

What does a heat pump cost installed in Upper Darby?

For a typical Upper Darby home, a professionally installed, ducted air source heat pump usually runs from $7,700–$16,500, but the actual price depends on a few specific features of your house: does it already have usable ductwork, or will that be extra? Is your electrical panel up to date? Older homes—especially those built before 1950—often need electrical upgrades or even new ductwork, which adds to the overall cost. Ductless mini-split heat pumps, for homes with no ducts or just single rooms needing conditioned air, come in at $3,100–$6,800 per zone installed. Complex multi-zone, ductless systems can climb past $10,000 if you’re serving most or all rooms. All work should include appropriate permits from the township.

What is EPA Section 608 certification?

EPA Section 608 certification is a federal requirement for anyone in Pennsylvania—or the rest of the country—who installs, services, or even checks the refrigerant charge on an air conditioner or heat pump. This universal rule means your installer must have a Section 608 card to legally handle refrigerant (including the new low-GWP types and older R-410A). Without it, a contractor cannot work on sealed systems, nor can they legally buy refrigerant. When getting bids for an HVAC replacement—where costs start at $4,800–$11,500 for a central AC in Upper Darby—ask to see who will actually be on site for the job and check their 608 status, not just the company’s sales team. It’s reasonable to request this before work begins for your own peace of mind and the system’s warranty.

How does southeastern Pennsylvania's climate affect system sizing?

Southeastern Pennsylvania is a heating-dominated climate with about 4,800 heating degree days annually and winter design temperatures down to 14 °F. This means your HVAC system—whether you’re installing a heat pump, a furnace, or both—must be sized with extra attention to heating needs as much as cooling. Oversizing leads to short cycling and poor humidity control, which isn’t good for either comfort or bills. Undersizing leaves entire rooms chilly on cold snaps. Older houses in Upper Darby (most built before 1980) were rarely air-sealed and sometimes lack insulation, which complicates sizing. Getting a proper, room-by-room load calculation as part of your estimate (even if you’re only replacing a central AC for $4,800–$11,500) is your best insurance against sizing mistakes here.

Is a heat pump a good choice for Upper Darby winters?

Thanks to advances in heat pump technology, a quality cold-climate model is now a practical option for an Upper Darby home, even with design temps as low as 14 °F. New ducted and ductless heat pumps can deliver full rated heat down to 5–10 °F, and with robust electrical service (common for homes built since 1950), you can cover nearly all your winter needs. In older, leakier houses, consider a dual fuel set-up: a heat pump for most days, but with a backup gas or oil furnace for sub-freezing cold snaps. Upfront cost for a heat-pump-based system runs from $7,700–$16,500 for a ducted install. If you’re all-electric—by choice or because there’s no available gas—heat pumps now offer real savings over oil or propane. Just make sure the installer sizes it for real Upper Darby weather, not just the catalog rating.

What size HVAC system does my Upper Darby home need?

Sizing depends on more than square footage. For Upper Darby homes—many built before 1980, with varied insulation and window types—a professional should calculate required system size based on actual “load” for your home’s orientation, construction, and leakage. The old “one ton for every 500 sq ft” rule of thumb can lead to oversizing, especially in attached rowhouses or twins. A load calculation is a computer model that maps how much heating or cooling you need on the area’s coldest and hottest days—about 14 °F and 92 °F—using Delaware County’s statistical weather. This calculation is part of any quality estimate, whether you’re replacing a heat pump ($7,700–$16,500) or installing new AC. It saves you from buying too much system and missing out on comfort or efficiency.

What HVAC tax credits are available to Upper Darby homeowners?

Federal tax credits are available for high-efficiency air source heat pumps, ductless mini-splits, and advanced central air, as long as the equipment hits minimum efficiency standards set by the IRS (these are published annually). In Pennsylvania, there are no separate, state-level tax credits, but utility incentives may also apply. PECO sometimes runs rebates for customers who install or upgrade to energy-efficient heat pumps, but these change year to year. Documentation is key: get a detailed invoice showing model number and efficiency, and keep a copy of your mechanical permit. Eligible heat pump installs (which can run from $7,700–$16,500 in Upper Darby) can claim up to a few thousand dollars in federal relief, depending on your filing. Consult your tax advisor for precise credit amounts and eligibility each year.

Do I need to be home during installation?

While you don’t technically have to be home for the entire length of your HVAC installation, it’s best practice to be present or have a trusted representative available for key milestones—especially equipment delivery, initial walkthrough, and final commissioning. In Upper Darby Township, inspectors may also need access for code sign-off once your system is installed. Being there helps address surprises—like hidden electrical issues or duct problems—in real time, and lets you ask commissioning questions before final payment. Schedule ahead and communicate with your contractor to minimize any disruption.
