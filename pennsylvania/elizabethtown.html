SEER2, HSPF2 and AFUE decoded

SEER2 stands for Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio 2, the updated standard for how efficiently an air conditioner or heat pump cools over a full season. The higher the SEER2, the less electricity you use per unit of cooling. HSPF2—Heating Seasonal Performance Factor 2—does the same for how efficiently a heat pump heats. AFUE, or Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency, is for furnaces and tells you the percentage of heat from fuel that gets delivered into the house. All of these replaced older SEER and HSPF ratings; numbers from before 2023 aren’t apples-to-apples anymore.

Here in Lancaster County, with more than four times as many heating degree days as cooling, HSPF2 and AFUE matter a lot more than the very highest SEER2. Paying for a SEER2 above 18 generally won’t pay back what you put in, given our moderate cooling season. But with heat pumps, spending a bit more for a high HSPF2 can cut your winter bills over time, especially if you use electric or heat pump heating as your primary system. Usually, once you’re in the top third for HSPF2 (9–11), going higher provides diminishing returns. For gas furnaces, 95%+ AFUE is the local minimum to consider, but springing for 98% only makes sense if you heat heavily. Stick with the efficiency sweet spot for the heating system; don’t overpay for high-end air conditioning.

Operating cost, not sticker price

When budgeting for a new HVAC system, the up-front sticker isn’t the whole story—operating cost deserves equal weight. Here’s how to break it down for an Elizabethtown home, given our 5,200 heating and 1,250 cooling degree days, and a typical winter design temperature near 12°F (from the local code).

Start with efficiency ratings: SEER2 for cooling, HSPF2 or AFUE for heating. Higher numbers save you more per dollar of energy input. Multiply your system’s output by the number of relevant degree days to estimate annual demand. Then find the "cost per kWh" or "cost per ccf" (natural gas) on your utility bill—most Elizabethtown homes receive electric service from PPL Electric Utilities.

Suppose your heat pump has a HSPF2 of 9.5, and you pay $0.16 per kWh. Estimate annual heating needs by multiplying your home’s square footage by average Btus per degree day, but realize this is a rough calculation—real bills fluctuate with weather, insulation, and duct leaks. If your ducts are losing 20% of heated air, your shiny high-efficiency unit performs no better than a mid-range one until you fix those leaks. This is why one household’s upgrade pays off in five years, while another’s savings vanish into the crawlspace.

Remember: rates change (often yearly), so calculate with your latest bill. Focus first on fixing leaks and updating controls before springing for top-tier ratings—it can make a bigger difference to total cost than chasing the highest SEER2 or HSPF2.

Service plans: worth it or not?

Many Elizabethtown homeowners are pitched annual HVAC service plans to keep equipment running. What do you really need? Changing your air filter at the start of each heating or cooling season, and verifying you’re using the right MERV rating for your blower, gives most of the return. Coil cleaning prevents summer breakdowns; a clogged or corroded coil kills efficiency. Clearing your condensate line stops water leaks, and checking the system refrigerant charge—never simply “topping it off”—identifies leaks before they get out of hand. If refrigerant is low, something is wrong and must be repaired.

Most annual service plans essentially prepay for routine visits. They’re worth considering if they include priority scheduling in peak seasons, honest diagnostics, and a fair discount on genuine repairs. Plans that force unnecessary add-ons and lock you into yearly fees aren’t good value. The worst are those covering filter changes but not the labor for real repairs.

Book maintenance in late winter or early fall to avoid peak demand—Elizabethtown’s rush hits first heatwaves and deep cold snaps, when emergencies always take priority.

The decision point on an aging system

Not every breakdown means a full replacement in Elizabethtown. Use a common-sense framework: If the system is still under 10 years old and it’s a single component that’s failed (like a capacitor or contactor), repair is usually the right call. Once your system is 10–15 years in, weigh the cost of repair—if it’s above one-third of the price of a whole new system, talk about replacement. Anything past 15 years with a serious failure (like a leaking coil, compressor, or heat exchanger) is usually due. Two “no-compromise” cases: a cracked heat exchanger (which is a carbon monoxide risk), or compressor failure out of warranty—both mean immediate replacement.

If you notice high utility bills but the system seems to be working, start by testing for duct and envelope leaks; lost air eats more money in Lancaster County’s heating season than any mechanical upgrade. Only move to replace if duct sealing doesn’t help and the system is genuinely inefficient.

Mechanical permits for HVAC work in Elizabethtown

In Pennsylvania, there is no statewide HVAC license for contractors. Residential work falls under the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), but it’s enforced or administered at the municipal level. For Elizabethtown and most of Lancaster County, that means mechanical permits and inspections come from your own township or borough—the state isn’t the default unless your municipality opted out entirely. For anything over $500, any residential HVAC contractor must be registered as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. Always verify HIC registration directly.

Anyone who handles refrigerant—such as when installing or servicing AC units or heat pumps—must have EPA Section 608 certification, which is federal law and applies everywhere. Workers’ compensation insurance is also required in Pennsylvania for all employers and can be verified through the statewide WCAIS system. If your project is in Lancaster city, both central AC and furnace installations require a mechanical permit; for basic tasks like air filter changes or programming thermostats, no permit is needed anywhere in Lancaster County.

Before you sign a contract (required by law on any job over $500), ask for proof of HIC registration, up-to-date insurance, and EPA 608 credentials for refrigerant work. Never proceed with a contractor who can’t provide those in writing.

Is an annual maintenance plan worth it? In Elizabethtown, the answer depends on what the plan covers and how diligent you are otherwise. What is the R-410A refrigerant phase-down? R-410A is the refrigerant used in most air conditioners and heat pumps installed in the last 15 years in Elizabethtown and across Pennsylvania. If you’re planning a major replacement (in the $4,800–$11,500 range), it’s smart to discuss future-proofing with your contractor; new systems installed after 2025 will increasingly use new refrigerants.
