Humidity and air quality in South Union homes

South Union homeowners know our region means battling winter dryness and summer humidity. With 5,600 heating degree days, the furnace or heat pump will dry out your house each winter—leaving lips cracked and hardwood floors shrinking. That’s why many locals install whole-house humidifiers. Just know, these need timely cleaning to keep mold and mineral buildup from clogging the pad or water line. They’re helpful, but not maintenance-free.

On the flip side, summer brings muggy days when an undersized or oversized AC can fail at dehumidifying. Oversized units chill the air too quickly and shut off before pulling moisture out, leaving you with a cool but clammy house. If your system never runs long enough to drain water from the coils, consider a dehumidifier rather than just chasing a bigger AC—don’t let anyone sell you on size alone.

Air filters are another area where it’s easy to overspend: higher MERV ratings take out finer dust and allergens, but they can restrict airflow and strain an older blower. Unless you have specific allergies or asthma needs, a filter in the MERV 8–11 range balances filtration and airflow for most South Union homes.

If you’ve just air-sealed your home or put in new windows, mechanical ventilation becomes more important than in those old leaky shells. An ERV or HRV can keep air fresh without giving up efficiency, but most homes here haven’t reached the point where these are necessary or cost-effective for the average family.

Heat pump or furnace: what makes sense in South Union

When choosing between a heat pump and a furnace in South Union, the answer hinges on our heating-dominated climate. With a winter design temperature of 8 °F and 5,600 heating degree days, even high-efficiency heat pumps have to work hard. The old idea that “heat pumps don’t work in Pennsylvania winters” isn’t true anymore—modern cold-climate units hold capacity well below freezing. But as outside temperatures dip toward single digits, heat pump output drops and efficiency tapers off, which is where either backup electric heat (less economical here) or a gas furnace comes in handy.

If your home has reliable natural gas service—which is common in Uniontown, Connellsville, and most South Union addresses—the installed and operating cost of a gas furnace plus central AC is tough to beat for reliable comfort. Dual fuel setups (heat pump with gas backup) make sense if you want efficient shoulder-season heating but still need reliable performance during Fayette County’s February cold snaps.

But for homes outside the natural gas grid—common in rural corners of the county—your options are a heat pump or sticking with expensive propane or fuel oil. Here, a heat pump almost always wins, as electric rates from West Penn Power usually outpace the per-BTU cost of delivered fuels, particularly for older, uninsulated houses.

An all-gas furnace may still be the right choice if your ductwork was sized for high airflow, your insulation isn’t great, or you just want the lowest install bill with reliable, simple heat. But for anyone planning a major renovation or seeking a future-proof system and lower carbon footprint, cold-climate heat pumps are no longer a gamble in South Union—just make sure your installer runs the numbers first.

Mechanical permits for HVAC work in South Union

Pennsylvania does not license HVAC contractors at the state level for residential work. Instead, contractors performing jobs over $500 must be registered through the state’s Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registry—a consumer protection system, not a technical license. Always ask contractors for their HIC registration number and verify it at hic.attorneygeneral.gov before you sign a contract.

Municipalities in Fayette County, including South Union, handle permit and code enforcement for residential HVAC work, with the City of Uniontown running its own separate office. For central system installations or any project requiring new mechanical or electrical connections, you need a mechanical permit. Swapping thermostats or changing filters does not, but major replacements do—always check with your local code office first.

For work involving refrigerant, ask if the technician on-site is EPA Section 608 certified; this is a federal requirement, not optional. Contractors must carry workers’ compensation insurance, which you can verify through Pennsylvania’s WCAIS system at wcais.pa.gov. Request both an insurance certificate and proof of workers’ comp before work starts—you do not want to be the default insurer if someone is hurt in your home. The bottom line: permitting, insured labor, and EPA-certified techs protect you more than any sticker on a van.

Running the numbers on South Union's climate

Before investing thousands in new HVAC equipment, it pays to understand how South Union’s 5,600 heating degree days and 1,050 cooling degree days translate into real-world operating costs. Start by looking at your current utility bills—West Penn Power is South Union’s dominant electric utility, and your rate is listed there in cents per kilowatt-hour (kWh). For gas, check your Peoples Natural Gas bill for the price per therm.

Here’s how to make a back-of-the-envelope comparison: a high-efficiency gas furnace might be rated at 96% AFUE. For every 100,000 BTU you buy, 96,000 BTU go into heat. A heat pump’s efficiency is stated as HSPF2 (e.g., 10), which tells you how many BTUs you get per watt-hour of electricity. Multiply your annual degree days by 24 (hours) and by the sizing rule-of-thumb for an average home, then break down costs based on your actual rate. For example, if electricity is 15 cents/kWh and your yearly heating load is 40 million BTU, you can estimate the annual operating cost by dividing that load by your system’s efficiency and then multiplying by your rate. For fuel oil or propane, the same math applies but convert gallons to BTUs.

Don’t trust these numbers blindly—rates change, and Fayette County homes from 1900–1940 tend to be leaky. Duct leaks, pinched runs, or missing insulation can make even the highest-rated gear perform badly in real terms. In the end, a mid-grade unit with tight ducts will outpace a top-tier unit if the air can’t get where it’s needed. Always have your installer inspect and test your ductwork as part of the bid, and don’t chase efficiency for its own sake if your delivery system is subpar.

Choosing an installer in Fayette County

In Fayette County, who installs your equipment matters far more than the sticker on the box—brand alone won’t fix a bad install. Given the county’s thin rural HVAC market outside Uniontown and Connellsville, it’s even more critical to verify your contractor’s credentials and process. Every residential HVAC contractor must have a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration through Pennsylvania's attorney general, but not a technical HVAC license. Refrigerant work requires the technician on your job to hold EPA Section 608 certification.

A reputable quote will include a real load calculation—never just “one ton per 500 square feet”—and will specify actual model numbers for all major equipment. At job completion, airflow, refrigerant charge, and thermostat controls should be measured and documented. If your installer doesn’t discuss duct condition, don’t assume your system will perform to its label rating.
