In tract neighborhoods like Hampden and Silver Spring, built out since the 1960s, most homes already have ductwork, allowing for direct replacement of central split systems or upgrades to new high-efficiency ducted air-source heat pumps rather than the high cost of new ducts.

Refrigerant rules and your existing system

In 2026, most new residential HVAC units sold in Pennsylvania use lower-global-warming-potential (GWP) refrigerants such as R-454B or R-32. This is the result of a nationwide shift away from high-GWP HFCs like R-410A. If your existing system uses R-410A—as most New Cumberland systems installed after 2010 do—you do not have to replace the whole system just because of this change. There is no law that makes your R-410A unit illegal or unserviceable.

Service and repairs continue as before. The catch: as the market moves away from R-410A in new units, the cost of R-410A refrigerant is rising. This is most relevant if your older system develops a refrigerant leak. Fixing a leak in a high-use cooling system can become expensive simply because the price of the refrigerant itself is rising as supply dries up and fewer contractors stock it.

If your system is running well, there’s no regulatory reason to swap it out. But if you face a major refrigerant repair—especially in a system nearing the end of its life—rising refrigerant cost can tip the scales toward replacement rather than repair. For most New Cumberland homeowners, this is an economic rather than a compliance-driven issue.

What you can actually claim on a heat pump here

In New Cumberland, financial incentives for heat pumps and other high-efficiency HVAC equipment break down into three main types: federal tax credits, local utility rebates, and possible state-level programs. The federal side (like the Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit) is a tax credit—meaning you claim it when you file your taxes the following year, lowering your tax bill rather than providing instant cash. Utility rebates, when available, are paid out faster—these either reduce your up-front invoice or pay you after installation and proof of purchase.

PPL Electric Utilities is the main electric provider for most of urban Cumberland County, and UGI Utilities provides natural gas in Carlisle and nearby communities. Specific rebate amounts and program rules change regularly. To get the most current information, start with your utility’s residential efficiency program web pages. For a more tailored list based on your zip code and equipment type, use the ENERGY STAR Rebate Finder. Don’t rely on numbers from general HVAC articles—each program updates yearly and rebates for heat pumps, ductless systems, and even insulation or thermostats shift frequently.

State-level programs in Pennsylvania tend toward temporary or targeted offerings rather than standing rebates, so always verify the current year’s options before making decisions. If you see a high rebate listed in an article, treat it as a starting point: actual eligibility and paperwork requirements matter almost as much as the advertised amount.

The central decision for a New Cumberland homeowner

Choosing between a furnace and a heat pump in New Cumberland is a real fork-in-the-road decision. This area is heating-dominated, with a winter design temperature of 12 °F and 5,200 annual heating degree days. That means most of your utility bill flows from heating, and any primary system must safely cover that 12 °F cold snap.

Modern cold-climate air-source heat pumps offer reliable heating well below freezing, thanks to inverter-driven compressors and improvements in refrigerant cycle control. Contrary to what many learned in the 1990s, the technology’s performance in the teens (°F) is not theoretical—and most units can heat to the high single digits before electric strip backup kicks in. However, output does drop off as outdoor temperatures plummet, justifying why the 12 °F mark from the code book matters locally. That’s why dual fuel—combining a high-efficiency furnace with a heat pump—remains popular in areas with gas service through UGI Utilities.

In rural Cumberland County, where natural gas isn’t readily available, the question shifts: comparing heat pumps to oil or propane dramatically shortens the payback, especially given the price swings and storage requirements tied to delivered fuels. For homes with on-site gas and minimal envelope upgrades, a high-efficiency furnace with a matched AC remains a conservative option—especially in older homes where heavy retrofitting is not practical.

Efficiency ratings and what they mean for your bill

Modern HVAC systems come rated in SEER2 (cooling efficiency) and HSPF2 (heating efficiency) for heat pumps. SEER2 reflects average seasonal cooling performance—higher means lower summer electricity costs. HSPF2 represents average seasonal heating efficiency, which is what matters most for Cumberland County, where heating makes up the lion’s share of energy use. Furnaces are rated by AFUE: the percentage of fuel turned into heat you can use indoors during a lab test season.

In a heating-dominated climate with 5,200 heating degree days, the premium for ultra-high SEER2 levels is rarely justified unless you run high summer loads. For most homeowners in New Cumberland, a SEER2 rating of 16–18 hits the sweet spot—paying sharply more for 20+ SEER2 usually doesn’t pay back in reasonable timelines. In contrast, investing in a heat pump with a HSPF2 near 10 can reduce your winter bill enough to justify the extra cost, especially if you lack natural gas.

Ratings changed from SEER/HSPF to SEER2/HSPF2 to reflect tougher lab testing as of 2023, so ignore apples-to-oranges comparisons with older numbers. Let a good contractor model your home’s actual energy use—and avoid any upsell that can’t show its payback, especially for central cooling extras you won’t use much here.

The decision point on an aging system

If your HVAC system is aging, the right move isn’t always replacement. First, look at the system’s age versus its expected life: most New Cumberland systems last 12–18 years, depending on maintenance and workload. If your repair is under 30%–40% of the price of a new system, and the unit is under 10 years old, a sound repair makes sense. Compressor or heat exchanger failures—especially in old systems—are tipping points where replacement should win. Units with R-410A are still fully serviceable, but as refrigerant prices rise, big leaks on old systems can make repair less economical. Also factor in your home’s actual needs: unless a new unit will save significant money on heating bills (less likely with central AC replacements in this climate), newer isn’t always smarter.

The two scenarios where replacement is immediate: a cracked heat exchanger (carbon monoxide risk) or a major compressor failure outside warranty on a system at or near its expected end of life. For high utility bills but otherwise working equipment, start with a duct leak or insulation test—many homes, especially those built before 1980, see more lost dollars through weak ducts than tired hardware.

What a Pennsylvania HVAC contractor must hold

Pennsylvania does not have a statewide license for residential HVAC contractors. Instead, for ordinary home installation or replacement, regulation falls mostly at the municipal level, not state. This means each township or city (like Carlisle) manages its own building and mechanical permits. There’s no master license in Harrisburg or anywhere in the commonwealth for standard residential HVAC work—you want a contractor registered with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General for Home Improvement Contracts (HIC registration) for jobs over $500, which you can verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov.

Anyone handling refrigerant—no matter the state—must hold EPA Section 608 certification. This is a federal requirement and absolutely non-negotiable for the tech actually working on your system. Mechanical permits are usually required for changeouts, and local code enforcement will handle inspections where applicable.

For insurance, contractors must carry workers’ compensation for all employees, and you can verify their coverage status using the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov). Always get a written contract for any job over $500; Pennsylvania law backs your right to cancel within three business days. This is one of the few states where the rules truly come from town hall—if a contractor can’t produce HIC paperwork, walk away.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in New Cumberland

Are duct sealing and insulation eligible for incentives? For many homeowners in New Cumberland, duct sealing and insulation can be a smart investment, especially since most homes were built before 1980 and often have leaky or poorly insulated ductwork. While there is no standing Pennsylvania state program that offers rebates just for duct improvements, some local utility programs run by PPL Electric Utilities include duct sealing or insulation as eligible measures under broader energy efficiency incentives. The best way to verify current offers is to check the PPL efficiency program website or the ENERGY STAR Rebate Finder for New Cumberland’s ZIP code, since these rebates change each year. Even if rebates are unavailable, duct improvements often cost between $300–$800 and can pay back in a heating-dominated climate like this one, where lost heat from ducts is common. If you’re upgrading a heat pump or central AC, proper duct work maximizes your investment, incentive or not.
Do I need to be home during installation? In New Cumberland, it’s strongly recommended that at least one adult be present at the start and end of a new HVAC installation, but many homeowners choose to be away during the work itself, especially for multi-day installations. For one-day jobs like a straightforward central AC replacement (which runs $5,000–$12,000), being home enables quick answers to access or thermostat questions. For larger projects, your contractor may need access to electrical panels, attics, or crawlspaces throughout the day, so arranging clear access and a contact phone number is usually sufficient. It’s very common in Pennsylvania for people to go about their day, but plan to do a walkthrough with the crew lead at the end: that’s when you’ll confirm comfort settings, thermostat operation, and any follow-up work. Municipal inspections, if required, almost never need you present—they’re arranged with the contractor directly.
What is EPA Section 608 certification? EPA Section 608 certification is a federal requirement for anyone in Pennsylvania who installs, repairs, or disposes of any residential or commercial HVAC equipment containing refrigerants. This law applies everywhere, including New Cumberland, and is not specific to any one state. The goal is to ensure that anyone handling refrigerants like R-410A, R-454B, or R-32 does so safely and without releasing them into the environment. If you hire an HVAC contractor for replacement or major repair (for example, a new ducted heat pump at $7,900–$17,000), ask to see evidence that the technician working with refrigerant is certified. This is non-negotiable: no reputable installer in Pennsylvania or any other state will lack this certification. It makes no difference whether the contractor is large or small.
Can I service my R-410A system after the refrigerant change? Yes, in New Cumberland and everywhere else in Pennsylvania, you can still have your R-410A system serviced, recharged, and repaired after the refrigerant transition. The phase-out only affects new equipment sales and manufacturing—it does not make your old system illegal or mean it can’t be maintained. The practical impact, however, is that R-410A refrigerant prices are rising, making large repairs or recharges more expensive, especially in a repair costing over $1,200. You are not forced to replace a working system. But if your system is 10–15 years old and develops a major refrigerant leak, the rising cost might make replacement more appealing. Your contractor can help you weigh the economics.
How long should an HVAC system last in New Cumberland? Typically, a modern central HVAC system in New Cumberland lasts 12–18 years, with the upper end possible for well-maintained units in homes with stable electrical service and regular filter changes. Systems installed in the post-1960 tract homes of Hampden or Silver Spring, if cared for, often reach 15+ years. The frequent freeze-thaw cycles and older ductwork in the borough’s vintage housing stock can shorten lifespan, especially if maintenance is skipped. Repairs under $1,200 are usually worth it for equipment under 10 years old, but once you approach 15 years—and especially if you’re facing compressor or heat exchanger failure—replacement makes more sense. Cold snaps down to 12 °F annually mean local systems work harder than in milder climates, so expect to replace a major component somewhere inside that time frame.
What does a heat pump cost installed in New Cumberland? For a full ducted air-source heat pump installation in New Cumberland, expect a total installed price of $7,900–$17,000, depending on your home’s size, duct condition, and specific cold-climate requirements. That range covers everything from single-story tract homes with existing ducts to larger, older homes needing some duct upgrades. If you have no central ducts, ductless mini-split systems run $3,200–$6,900 per single zone, but a whole-home multi-zone system can go higher. The low end of these bands generally covers replacing older equipment with similar size and type, while the upper end includes challenging installs, electrical upgrades, or whole-duct replacement. Always ask for a full quote including electrical and permit work—unexpected add-ons are common in the area’s older homes.
How much does a new HVAC system cost in New Cumberland? The price of a new HVAC system in New Cumberland depends on whether you have working ductwork and what type of equipment you select. For most homes with solid existing ducts, a central AC replacement runs $5,000–$12,000 installed. Upgrading to a ducted air-source heat pump for year-round comfort is typically $7,900–$17,000. If your home needs a complete new duct system—common in older single-family properties—the investment is higher, usually $8,900–$18,000 extra, depending on the building’s layout and access. The top end of every price band reflects upgrades for efficiency, controls, or significant electrical work. Always ask your contractor to assess your existing ducts before committing to a full replacement—many retrofits in post-1960 homes can reuse or simply upgrade what’s already there.
Which utility serves New Cumberland? Most New Cumberland homeowners receive electric service from PPL Electric Utilities, which serves the urbanized parts of Cumberland County including the borough itself and nearby townships. If you’re considering a new heat pump or high-efficiency AC (ranges from $7,900–$17,000 depending on system type), check PPL’s residential efficiency program page for current rebate offers. For natural gas, UGI Utilities is the main provider in Carlisle and many surrounding areas, but further west in the county, gas service drops off and propane or fuel oil becomes common. It’s important to confirm which fuels are available at your address before making a major system decision, as this will affect both operating costs and incentive eligibility.
Is it worth switching from oil or propane to a heat pump? In New Cumberland and much of western Cumberland County, where natural gas service is limited, switching from oil or propane to a modern cold-climate heat pump is often financially smart. Delivered fuels can be unpredictable in price and always involve tank fills and annual maintenance. A full ducted heat pump installation is typically $7,900–$17,000, and many homeowners can recoup the investment via lower operating costs and available federal tax credits. Payback is especially quick if your current oil or propane furnace is more than 15 years old, as electric heat pumps are far more efficient and avoid the delivery hassle. The real savings depend on your home’s insulation and layout, so always combine this upgrade with a look at ducts and sealing—sometimes, a little tightening shaves more off your bill than new hardware alone.
