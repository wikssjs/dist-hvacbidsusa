Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, HVAC work isn’t just “best practice”—it’s regulated. Pennsylvania requires HVAC contractors to hold a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration through the Pennsylvania Attorney General’s Office, and you should verify it before signing anything. If gas appliances are involved (like furnace replacement), the installing technician must hold an active PA gas piping licence. For air conditioning and heat pump work, refrigerant handling requires EPA Section 608 certification—ask for the technician’s EPA 608 card.

Mechanical permits are required by most PA municipalities for HVAC replacement and new installations, but requirements vary by township or borough. Typically, a permit is not required for like-for-like filter changes or thermostat swaps (when no wiring/line-voltage or major components are altered). Specific cases—adding equipment, altering fuel type, changing duct layouts, or replacing the outdoor/indoor coil section with changes to electrical—are much more likely to require a permit and inspection sign-off.

For rebates and incentives, check the PA Homeowner Rebate program and utility rebates (PPL Electric, PECO, Columbia Gas) for qualifying high-efficiency heat pumps and furnaces under IRA Inflation Reduction Act programs. ENERGY STAR certified equipment may also qualify for federal tax credits up to 30% under Section 25C.

How to verify in Shrewsbury step-by-step: (1) confirm the contractor’s PA HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov; (2) request the technician’s EPA 608 certificate; (3) ask for a current certificate of insurance; (4) request proof of workers’ compensation coverage (or a valid exemption, if applicable); and (5) ensure the company will pull required mechanical permits through the local building office before work starts.

Heat pump vs. furnace + AC — what makes sense in Shrewsbury?

Shrewsbury homeowners are often deciding between a heat pump system and the traditional combination of a furnace plus central AC. Modern cold-climate air source heat pumps (CC-ASHP) can operate efficiently down to about -13°F, which makes them viable across much of York County. That’s why I focus on system match: if your ducts are leaky or undersized, a furnace/AC replacement may still “work” but struggle to distribute air evenly, which can lead to cycling and comfort issues. A heat pump can be a great fit when your home is sealed/insulated enough that the indoor airflow and heat transfer stay consistent during cold snaps. On the other hand, if the home has limited duct capacity, adding sealed ductwork, a properly sized blower setup, and airflow balancing can be the difference between a comfortable system and one that feels weak.

Here’s where the money makes sense. If you’re choosing between a ducted heat pump installed around $6,500–$14,000 and a gas furnace replacement plus central AC that can total $4,500–$18,000, the justify-it factor is how often you actually need emergency heating and how efficient your duct delivery is. In a newer, better-insulated setup, the heat pump advantage often shows up quickly. In older duct-heavy homes, correcting airflow and insulation first can preserve performance no matter what equipment you pick.

How to choose a HVAC installation contractor in Shrewsbury

Choosing the right HVAC contractor in Shrewsbury comes down to credentials, transparency, and proof of workmanship—not just a competitive price. Start with licensing checks. Pennsylvania requires a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration through the Attorney General’s Office; verify it at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For duct and system quality, ask whether the contractor participates in recognized training and whether their techs hold IICRC (as applicable to their work type). If the job includes refrigerant handling, confirm the technician holds EPA Section 608 certification—request to see the card or documentation during quoting.

Next, get 2–3 quotes that are itemized. A proper quote breaks out labor and materials (equipment, indoor/outdoor components, electrical disconnects, line-set, drain components, duct sealing parts, permit fees, and airflow testing). Avoid lump-sum only proposals when your home may need return corrections or duct repair.

Read the scope carefully. What’s excluded? For example: does the contractor include duct sealing and balancing, electrical upgrades, removal/disposal, and combustion or static pressure testing? For warranty coverage, separate the workmanship warranty length from the equipment warranty and ask who honors each. Payment should follow a sensible schedule: never pay more than 10–15% upfront, and request a holdback until the job is complete and performance testing is done. Finally, insist on a written arrival estimate and an achievable completion window—lead times for full replacements can affect scheduling in peak periods.

What is a SEER2 rating and why does it matter in Shrewsbury?

SEER2 is a measure of an air conditioner’s cooling efficiency—how effectively it converts electricity into cooling output—under current testing standards. In Shrewsbury and across York County, it matters because you have real summer cooling demand, plus the system must run long enough during humid stretches to maintain indoor comfort and humidity control. A higher SEER2 (for example, 16–20 SEER2 in many central AC replacement scenarios) generally costs more upfront, but it can reduce operating costs over time—especially when combined with good airflow and clean, properly sized ductwork. In older homes (46.2% pre-1980), duct leakage can cause conditioned air to “go missing,” forcing the system to run longer; that effectively undermines the efficiency you’re paying for. That’s why I treat SEER2 as one piece of the puzzle. Your quote should also account for installation variables: correct refrigerant charge, airflow testing, duct sealing where needed, and proper return sizing. If your plan includes air conditioning plus heating, confirm the full system efficiency and controls strategy, not just the outdoor unit sticker.
