What a Pennsylvania HVAC contractor must hold

Pennsylvania does not issue a statewide HVAC contractor license for residential work. Instead, the state relies on the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), which is enforced at the municipal level for residential projects. In Selinsgrove Borough itself, permits and mechanical inspections are handled by the Selinsgrove Borough Codes Enforcement Office, and you’ll need a permit for any central HVAC system replacement or installation. Out in township areas, you’ll deal with the township or their designated code official. There’s no universal state trade license—only registration as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) is required for residential contracts over $500. You can verify HIC registration status at hic.attorneygeneral.gov before signing any contract.

Anyone handling refrigerant—every legitimate installer replacing or charging your AC or heat pump—must hold EPA Section 608 certification. This requirement is federal and applies throughout Pennsylvania, regardless of what municipality you live in.

If the contractor has employees, Pennsylvania law requires them to carry workers’ compensation insurance, which you can verify through WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). Always ask for a certificate of insurance before work begins; this is protection for you as well as them. For work on homes built before 1978, your contractor should also be compliant with the federal EPA Lead RRP Rule.

Written contracts are mandatory for projects over $500 under state law, and you cannot legally waive your homeowner’s insurance deductible. You can walk away from a contract within three business days thanks to the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act.

Efficiency ratings and what they mean for your bill

There are two main efficiency ratings you’ll see: SEER2 for cooling and HSPF2 for heat pumps in heating mode. SEER2 (Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio 2) measures cooling efficiency, while HSPF2 (Heating Seasonal Performance Factor 2) rates a heat pump’s ability to deliver heat using electricity. Higher numbers are better—more comfort for each dollar of electric use.

In Snyder County, heating outweighs cooling by a wide margin: 6,300 annual heating degree days to just 750 cooling degree days. That means HSPF2 is the more important rating, especially if you’ll use the system for significant home heating. Paying a premium for top-end SEER2 is rarely justified here since you’ll only rely on AC for a short part of the year. On the heating side, moving from a standard 8 HSPF2 to a 9 or 10 rating can pay back over time with lower electric use—especially valuable at our low winter temperatures (2 °F design temp).

Be aware: these ratings changed recently—older sources cite SEER and HSPF. SEER2 and HSPF2 use new testing methods that better reflect real-world conditions, so don’t compare them directly to older numbers.

The sweet spot here is mid-high HSPF2 for heat pumps. For cooling, a 16–18 SEER2 system is efficient enough for Selinsgrove; going higher brings little benefit for the extra cost in most homes.

Mini-splits and zoning in Selinsgrove homes

Ductless mini-split systems offer strong advantages in Selinsgrove, mainly due to the nature of our local housing stock. Most Snyder County homes were built between the 1950s and 1980s. Many of these ranches and split-levels either lack central ducts entirely or have ductwork sized just for heating, making ducted AC or heat pump installs expensive or disruptive. Attics, additions, and some finished basements often have no ducts at all.

Ductless mini-splits shine in these problem spots. Installing a single-zone system—one outdoor unit matched to one indoor head—in an addition or an area that never gets comfortable is straightforward and less invasive than adding ductwork. Multi-zone setups (one outdoor compressor serving several heads in different rooms) are possible, but each head’s placement needs to be planned carefully for coverage and aesthetics. Heads are most often wall-mounted and can be visually intrusive, which is a common complaint. Floor or ceiling units are available but less common and typically cost more to install. Condensate lines must drain properly—sometimes this requires a small pump, and careful installation is crucial to prevent future leaks or freezing in unconditioned spaces.

A caution: multi-zone mini-split systems on a single outdoor unit can struggle to heat or cool each room evenly if only some zones call for conditioning. Separate single-zone systems provide superior comfort and are often more efficient, even if initial installation costs are higher. For a single-room addition or retrofit, a ductless mini-split costs $2,900–$6,400 installed in this market.

Service plans: worth it or not?

Most Selinsgrove HVAC service plans bundle annual cleaning and inspections, but what you really need is much simpler. At minimum, change your air filter regularly—every 1–3 months depending on pet hair, dust and your system’s airflow sensitivity. Use the MERV rating your blower was designed for; too high a MERV can strain older motors and cut airflow, especially in older duct systems common in Snyder County homes.

Annual coil cleaning, making sure the condensate drain stays clear, and a full refrigerant charge check are all worthwhile. If a system is low on refrigerant, you have a leak; topping it up year after year is not maintenance, it’s ignoring a failure. Any “service” plan that simply adds refrigerant at every visit is not worth the paper it’s written on.

Skip plans that bundle lots of “free” parts, as they’re often priced higher than actual replacement costs. Instead, pay for a basic annual tune-up before the main season (spring for AC, fall for heat pumps/furnaces), when technicians have time to do it right—before they’re pulled away for emergency calls in the first heat wave or cold snap.

Running the numbers on Selinsgrove's climate

In north-central Pennsylvania, it’s heating—not cooling—that drives your annual utility bill. Selinsgrove has roughly 6,300 heating degree days and 750 cooling degree days each year, and the electric utility here is PPL Electric Utilities. If you want to understand your system’s real operating cost, you need three things: the system’s efficiency (HSPF2 for heat pumps, SEER2 for AC), the number of degree days, and your electric rate (check your bill for cost per kilowatt-hour).

For example, let’s look at a heat pump upgrade. Suppose your current heat pump is rated at 8 HSPF2 and you’re considering upgrading to a 10 HSPF2 model. If your house needs about 30 million BTUs (roughly what 6,300 degree days represent for a typical ranch), the new unit will use 20% less power for heating. If your rate is $0.14 per kWh (check your actual bill), you’d save about $250–$400 a year—assuming your ducts are tight and insulated.

On cooling, a new central AC at 18 SEER2 is already efficient for our brief hot spells. Going to 20 SEER2 might save only $20–$50 a year—hardly worth the premium unless you run your system constantly.

The biggest wild card is ductwork. Leaks or poorly insulated ducts undo predicted savings, and no system can make up for heat lost before it ever reaches your rooms. Spend on sealing and improving ducts if you suspect problems—dollar for dollar, it pays back faster and more reliably than ultra-high equipment efficiency.
