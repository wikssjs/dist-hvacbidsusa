Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, HVAC work isn’t “anything goes.” For homeowners in South Hanover, start by confirming the contractor is properly registered and the job is permitted when required. Pennsylvania requires HVAC contractors to hold a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration through the Pennsylvania Attorney General’s Office. For gas appliances, the installing technician must hold an active PA gas piping license. If the work involves refrigeration (most AC and heat pump installs), the technician handling refrigerant must be EPA Section 608 certified—ask to see the EPA 608 card.

Most PA municipalities also require mechanical permits for HVAC replacement and new installation. A permit is typically not required for like-for-like filter changes or thermostat swaps, but replacement of major equipment (furnace, air conditioner, heat pump, or any refrigerant circuit work) generally triggers permitting. Because permit rules can vary by township or borough, verify specifics with your local municipality.

For affordability, check available programs: the PA Homeowner Rebate program and utility rebates (PPL Electric, PECO, Columbia Gas) may apply to qualifying high-efficiency heat pumps and furnaces under Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) pathways. ENERGY STAR-certified equipment may also qualify for federal tax credits up to 30% under Section 25C.

How to verify credentials step-by-step: (1) look up the contractor’s PA HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov; (2) ask which technician will perform refrigerant work and request their EPA 608 card; (3) request a certificate of insurance showing liability and workers’ compensation; and (4) confirm the contractor provides written permit handling if your job requires it.

How to choose a HVAC installation contractor in South Hanover

For AC and heat pump refrigerant handling, request the technician’s EPA Section 608 certification card before work begins.

Next, get 2–3 written, itemized quotes. Ask for a breakdown that separates labor and materials and lists key components (matching blower size, line-set details, condensate parts, thermostat/model numbers, and electrical requirements). Read the scope line-by-line: what is included, what’s excluded (for example, ductwork sealing, electrical panel upgrades, or permit fees), and how the contractor will handle jobsite conditions. If the contractor is doing duct-related corrections, confirm it’s in the written scope and not an “option later.”

Warranties matter. Look for a clear workmanship warranty length (not just equipment) and distinguish that from equipment warranties. Avoid large upfront payments: pay no more than 10–15% upfront, and hold back the remainder until the system is completed, started, and tested. Finally, require a timeline in writing—both an arrival estimate and the completion window—especially since peak-season installs in the region can run 1–3 weeks.
