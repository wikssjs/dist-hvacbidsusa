SEER2, HSPF2 and AFUE decoded

SEER2 (Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio 2) tells you how efficiently a cooling system turns electricity into cold air across the full cooling season. HSPF2 (Heating Seasonal Performance Factor 2) measures a heat pump’s heating efficiency over a winter, under updated test conditions. AFUE (Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency) is the standard for furnaces, showing what percent of fuel energy becomes usable heat.

For Swarthmore and the rest of Delaware County, heating needs outstrip cooling: 4,800 heating degree days dwarf the 1,450 cooling degree days typical for southeastern Pennsylvania. That means HSPF2—or AFUE, for gas and oil systems—should drive your efficiency upgrade decisions. Paying a premium for an AC with the highest SEER2 rarely pays back here, while a higher HSPF2 for a heat pump can be smart if you use it as your primary heat. Top-tier SEER2 units (above 18) rarely save enough on summer bills to justify their higher upfront cost in this region’s climate.

If most of your winter heat still comes from gas or oil, spend the efficiency dollars on tightening ducts and insulating where it counts, rather than maxing out AC or even heat pump SEER2 numbers. And remember: SEER2 and HSPF2 replaced the older SEER/HSPF ratings—numbers from older articles or manuals aren’t directly comparable, so always check which scale you’re seeing.

What construction era means for your system

Nearly all Swarthmore homes were built before 1980, with most dating even earlier (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). That tells you a few things up front: original ductwork, if it exists, was sized for furnaces and may be poorly sealed or insulated. Many area homes—including rowhouses and twins—were designed for radiators, not forced air, meaning no original ducts at all. If you’re considering an all-electric heat pump replacement, keep in mind that older homes might also need an upgraded electrical panel to provide enough amperage.

The typical insulation and window package in these houses is well below modern code, pushing up both heating and cooling demand. Gas appliances relying on a masonry chimney may face venting issues; lining or upgrading the flue can be required at replacement time. On the cooling side, irregular attic or crawlspace ducts make for uneven comfort and wasted power, so testing and sealing are worth their cost.

Bottom line: expect some work beyond “just swapping the box” when it comes to HVAC replacement in Swarthmore. Ductless mini-splits or high-velocity systems often make better sense than cutting holes for standard ductwork in homes that were never meant to have it.

The federal tax credit reduces the amount of tax you owe when you file your next return, while rebates—usually from utilities—lower the price of your system or installation invoice up front.

If your home is served by PECO Energy Company for electric or gas, check their efficiency program page before starting any project. There are occasionally state-level initiatives, but Pennsylvania’s programs for residential HVAC shift with budget cycles and are not always open for new applicants. Never bank on an incentive until you’ve checked the current details for your address and system type. Always ask your contractor to price jobs both with and without anticipated rebates, so you’re not blindsided if amounts or eligibility change.

The decision point on an aging system

When deciding between repairing and replacing an HVAC system in Swarthmore, start with age and repair cost versus full replacement. Most central ACs and furnaces run 12–18 years in this climate; heat pumps trend closer to 10–15 if used as main heat. If your diagnosis is an isolated component—a blower, board, or contactor—on a unit under 10 years old, repair almost always makes sense.

If a repair climbs over a third of a full system replacement, or your unit is past 15 years, consider the benefits of new equipment—especially if it uses outdated refrigerant or struggles on extreme days. In Delaware County’s heating-dominated climate, bigger efficiency gains come from tightening ductwork and air sealing rather than maxing out equipment specs. Do not feel pressured to replace a system just because it is out of warranty or not “high-efficiency” by current marketing.

Reluctantly but clearly, there are only two true mandatory replacements: a cracked heat exchanger (a carbon monoxide risk), and a major compressor failure in a system already near or past its service life.

Separating installers from equipment sellers

When you get HVAC bids in Swarthmore or anywhere in Delaware County, installation quality trumps brand. The same furnace or heat pump can run beautifully—or struggle for years—depending on the care taken with sizing, ductwork, and start-up. Demand that your installer backs up claims with documented steps, not just a flashy brand and high efficiency label.

Start by verifying contractor status: check HIC registration at the Pennsylvania registry and EPA 608 certification for any technician handling refrigerant. Every real quote should include a load calculation (not just “same size as before”), actual equipment model numbers, and a written scope. Commissioning—measured airflow, duct static pressure, and refrigerant charge—should be performed and documented at startup; don’t accept a handshake or a promise that “it’s all good.”

  • Confirm HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov for any contractor.
  • Ask if the technician on-site is EPA Section 608 certified for refrigerant handling.
  • Require a room-by-room load calculation for sizing, not rules of thumb.
  • See full equipment model and serial numbers on the proposal, not just BTU or tonnage.
  • Contract lists all labor, equipment, demolition, and haul-away specifics.
  • Get a copy of the mechanical permit, if your township requires one.
  • Proof of insurance and workers’ compensation—for your address—must be verifiable; use wcais.pa.gov if there’s any doubt.
  • Ask about commissioning steps: airflow, refrigerant, static pressure checked after install.
  • No payment in full until the work passes final inspection (if required).
  • Insist on references from jobs in older Swarthmore or Delaware County homes, not just recent subdivisions.
  • Clarify warranty coverage for both equipment and labor, in writing.
  • Never accept ballpark figures without a thorough home visit.

In a market as saturated as Delaware County, the worst red flag is a contractor who can start “tomorrow” or quotes wildly below the price bands shown above. Quality outfits are typically booked in advance, especially at the peak of a heat wave or cold snap.

What a Pennsylvania HVAC contractor must hold

Pennsylvania does not license HVAC contractors at the state level for residential work. Instead, contractors doing residential HVAC jobs—including those in Swarthmore—must register for Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) status with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General for projects over $500. This registration is not a test of technical competence but is legally required, and you can check it yourself at the state’s verification website listed above.

Actual permits and code inspections are issued locally; each municipality around Delaware County, including Swarthmore, runs its own code office. For new installations or replacements, expect to need a mechanical permit. The technician actually working on your system must hold EPA Section 608 certification to handle refrigerant, which is a federal requirement and not optional.

Contractors must carry workers’ compensation insurance—whether through the private market or self-insured—and you can verify coverage using the WCAIS system. Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act also guarantees you a written contract for work over $500 and a 3-day right to cancel. Don’t agree to anything without these basics. No extra trade licensure is required for residential HVAC or ductwork aside from local permits and EPA 608.

Frequently asked questions — HVAC installation in Swarthmore

Why do HVAC quotes in Swarthmore vary so much? Contractors serving Swarthmore offer a wide range of HVAC bids because older homes here differ dramatically in ductwork condition, insulation, and access for installation. About 96.9% of homes pre-date 1980, so what sounds like a simple swap on paper may involve unexpected repairs or duct retrofitting. Some contractors only quote for replacing the equipment—others include duct sealing, wiring, or fixes to venting that older Swarthmore homes often need. Add in differences in equipment choices, brands, markup, warranty coverage, and labor quality (installation matters far more than brand), and price swings are common. You may see everything from $5,600–$13,500 for a central AC if ductwork is solid, to much higher if full replacement is needed. Always ask what’s included and compare apples to apples—a cheap quote that skips permits or ductwork will cost more down the line.
Why are my Swarthmore energy bills high with a new system? Even with new equipment, many Swarthmore homeowners find their energy bills remain stubbornly high. The reason is rarely the HVAC unit itself—instead, it’s usually duct leakage, poor insulation from the home’s pre-1980 construction, or outdated windows. Most local homes have leaky or uninsulated ducts, which can lose up to one third of heated or cooled air before it even reaches your rooms, especially if the ducts run through attics or crawlspaces. Insist on duct sealing and consider adding insulation if your house is still drafty. Sometimes a targeted investment of $300–$800 in these “invisible” improvements pays back faster than the jump to a higher-efficiency system. Addressing the envelope is almost always a smarter first step in Delaware County’s heating-weighted climate.
Are duct sealing and insulation eligible for incentives? In most years, both duct sealing and attic insulation upgrades are eligible for some rebates or tax credits, but the programs and dollar amounts change annually. In Swarthmore, you’ll need to check PECO Energy Company’s efficiency program page for current offerings and any special requirements. Federal tax credits sometimes include duct sealing as part of a comprehensive home performance upgrade. The easiest way to check what’s available right now: use the ENERGY STAR rebate finder tool and look up your ZIP code. No contractor or article—including this one—can guarantee a rebate figure in advance, because utilities shift programs as regulations and budgets change. Still, even if the direct incentive is smaller than on a full system (say, $300–$800), the savings in energy bills can be substantial, especially in Delaware County’s older homes.
How much does ductwork add to an HVAC installation? If your Swarthmore home has no usable ducts—common in pre-war twins and rowhouses—adding a full duct system is the single largest upgrade you can make, but it does come at a premium. Installing new, properly sized and insulated ducts typically adds $10,000–$20,500 to the full job, bringing your total well above the cost of just the furnace, heat pump, or air conditioner. Final cost depends on the number of floors, finished basements, retrofitting around old framing, and the amount of wall and ceiling patching required. In some historic homes, ductless systems at $3,600–$7,900 per zone can offer relief for additions or upstairs bedrooms without cutting up the house for full ducting.
Do I need dual fuel in southeastern Pennsylvania? Dual fuel systems—which pair a heat pump with a gas or oil furnace—are worth considering in Swarthmore if your home already has gas service and you want both efficient cooling and reliable backup heat. In a heating-heavy county like Delaware, where winter design temperatures drop to 14 °F and heating degree days average 4,800, a standard air source heat pump may struggle on the coldest nights unless you install a true cold-climate model. Dual fuel lets you use the heat pump most of the year, saving on gas, and automatically switches to the furnace when it’s more efficient or comfortable. Installations range from $9,000–$19,000 depending on duct and controls complexity. For homes without gas, backup is usually electric resistance heat, but that’s costly in real cold spells.
Who inspects HVAC work in Swarthmore? HVAC installations and replacements in Swarthmore fall under local code enforcement, not state inspectors. Each municipality in Delaware County—including Swarthmore—has its own code office and process. For most systems beyond simple thermostat swaps, you’ll need a mechanical permit, and a local code official will do the inspection once the work is done. Expect them to check for proper clearances, combustion air, electrical safety, venting, and that the correct equipment was installed. Always ask your HVAC contractor to pull the required permits—if they do not, that’s a red flag. Inspection fees and timing vary by township, but this process helps ensure your $5,600–$13,500 or greater investment is safely installed and eligible for warranties and insurance.
What MERV filter rating should I use? Swarthmore owners in older homes should balance filtration with airflow. A MERV 8 filter is usually safe for most pre-1980 duct systems—the higher you go, the more it can stress the blower or reduce air delivery, especially if the existing ducts are narrow or leaky. MERV 11 and above are fine if anyone in the house has allergies, but you should have your installer confirm static pressure with a manometer after installing a higher-rated filter. Overloading an older blower can actually cost you in both bills and comfort. For most local systems, budget a standard filter replacement every 2–3 months—at just $300–$800 annually for routine service, this helps maintain efficiency and indoor air quality.
Do older Swarthmore homes need ductwork replaced? In a town where almost all homes were built before 1980, it’s common to find original ductwork that’s either leaky, poorly insulated, or wrongly sized—especially if your house was converted from radiators. Complete duct replacement is warranted if you’re experiencing uneven temps, noisy operation, or visible rust and gaps in the runs. Modern duct installation runs $10,000–$20,500, but partial repair and targeted sealing ($300–$800) often fixes the biggest issues at far lower cost in the short term. Have your contractor inspect with a camera or blower door test before committing to a full redo—most Swarthmore owners find a stepwise fix yields most of the benefit.
What is commissioning and why does it matter? Commissioning is the technical start-up phase where your new HVAC system is tested and adjusted to run as designed in your particular home. For Swarthmore households, where ducts can be leaky and houses drafty, proper commissioning means the installer measures airflow, checks refrigerant pressures, verifies thermostat operation, and ensures renovations haven’t blocked any ducts. Without it, your new investment (which could be anywhere from $9,000–$19,000 for a heat pump or furnace install) may underperform, waste energy, or even fail early. Commissioning is what separates a true installation company from an equipment “swap shop”—always ask for documentation of final readings, and hold part of payment until you’re confident your system is running at its best.
